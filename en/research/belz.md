## 1. Chronological overview

### 1.1 Founding of the Belz court (1815/1817)

R. Shalom Rokeach (1779–1855), one of the disciples of R. Yaakov Yitzchak Horowitz, the "Seer of Lublin," settled in Bełz around 1815, and in 1817 — on the recommendation of the Seer of Lublin — accepted the town's rabbinate [1][2][3]. He thereby founded the Belz court, which quickly drew thousands of Hasidim from Poland, Galicia and Hungary [1]. He was called by his Hasidim "Sar Shalom" [2][3], was known both as a wonder-worker and as a Torah scholar, and maintained close relations with the non-Hasidic rabbis of Galicia of his time [1].

**[General background]** R. Shalom Rokeach was a descendant (grandson) of R. Eliezer Rokeach, author of "Maaseh Rokeach," hence the Rokeach family name identified with the Belz dynasty.

### 1.2 Construction of the Great Synagogue (1834)

In 1834 the Great Synagogue was built in Bełz — a monumental structure in the Moorish style [2]. The synagogue became a symbol of Jewish spiritual life not only in Galicia but in all of Eastern Europe [2].

### 1.3 The 19th century: Belz as the center of Hasidism in Galicia

**[General background]** In the 19th century Belz became the spiritual and intellectual center of Galician Hasidism [2]; the Belz yeshiva acquired a great reputation in the region [3].

### 1.4 Continuity of the dynasty (1855–1926–1957)

- **R. Shalom Rokeach** — founder of the court, served 1817–1855 (died September 10, 1855) [1][2].
- **R. Yehoshua Rokeach** (1825–1894), his youngest son, served as the second rebbe 1855–1894; under his leadership the Belz court expanded into the largest Hasidic court in Galicia [1][4].
- **R. Yissachar Dov Rokeach** (1854 – October 29, 1926), son of R. Yehoshua, served as the third rebbe from 1894 until his death in 1926; he was a recognized leader of Galician Jewry, rabbi of the town of Bełz, and also headed the "Machzikei Hadas" movement [1][5].
- **R. Aharon Rokeach** (December 19, 1880 – August 18, 1957), eldest son of R. Yissachar Dov, served as the fourth rebbe from 1926 [1][6]. In his time the Belz community was destroyed in the Holocaust (see chapter 1.5), and he himself was saved by fleeing via Vizhnitz, the Bochnia ghetto and the Kraków ghetto, in disguise, until he reached the Land of Israel in February 1944 [6]. His nine children and their families perished in the Holocaust, among them his eldest son Moshe, who was burned alive in the synagogue in Przemyślany [6].

### 1.5 The destruction of Bełz (1939–1942)

- **October 10, 1939:** the Germans took control of Bełz, by force of the agreement between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union; most of the local Jews had already fled eastward before the entry of the German forces [3]. A forced-labor camp was established in Bełz, in which Jews deported from nearby localities were also concentrated [3].
- **The Great Synagogue:** according to a documented photograph titled "Belz, Great synagogue, 1941" (see chapter 6), the synagogue was burned by 1941; according to another source, the most important Jewish heritage buildings — the Great Synagogue, the beit midrash and the Talmud Torah — were destroyed by the Nazis in 1942 [3]. The two details do not necessarily contradict each other (burning in 1941, physical demolition of the remains in 1942), but the sources found do not set out a single consistent sequence of events, and this should be noted as an inconsistency between the sources.
- **May 1942:** 1,540 Jews were still living in Bełz [3].
- **June 2, 1942:** about 1,000 of them were marched about 60 km to Hrubieszów, and from there deported to the Sobibór extermination camp [3].
- **September 1942:** the 504 remaining Jews were deported and murdered in the same manner [3]. With this the Belz community was annihilated completely.

## 2. The structure of the court and the custom of the pilgrimage to the Rebbe

**[General background]** A Hasidic court such as Belz operated around the figure of the rebbe as a center of spiritual, halachic and communal authority: Hasidim would travel to the Rebbe for the festivals ("festival days at the court"), take part in "tishen" (public meals at which the rebbe delivered words of Torah), and present "kvitlekh" (notes of request for blessing and prayer) and "pidyonot" — a practice documented explicitly later in the Rokeach dynasty (for example with R. Mordechai of Bilgoray) and reflecting the accepted custom in the Belz courts [4].

The Belz court was known for the emphasis its founder, R. Shalom Rokeach, placed on the observance of practical commandments and on acts of loving-kindness — and not only on depth of Talmudic study — as a central value of his Hasidic path [2]. Later in the 19th century, under the leadership of R. Yehoshua Rokeach, the Belz court grew into the largest Hasidic court in Galicia [4], and under R. Yissachar Dov Rokeach the head of the court became known as a "recognized leader of Galician Jewry" [5] — a designation attesting to an authoritative standing that extended well beyond the bounds of the town of Bełz itself. The "Machzikei Hadas" movement, which R. Yissachar Dov headed, was the court's organizational-political arm in the struggle against the Haskalah and for Orthodoxy in Galicia [1][5].

## 3. The connection to Eastern Galicia and Rawa Ruska

**[General background]** Bełz lay on the edge of Eastern Galicia, close to the border with the Congress Kingdom (the Chełm district), and this location contributed to drawing Hasidim both from Galicia itself and from across the Russian-Polish border.

The town of Rawa Ruska, near Bełz (about 25 km), was subject to the influence of the Belz court and the Rokeach dynasty: "The Hasidic movement succeeded in striking roots in Rawa Ruska, and the entire town was under the influence of the Belz court and the Rokeach Hasidic dynasty" [4]. At the end of the 19th century Jews constituted a majority of Rawa Ruska's population — 3,878 souls (60%) in 1880, and 6,112 souls (57%) in 1910 [4] — so that the influence of the Belz court applied to a large and physically close Jewish community.

No source was located in this search detailing the mechanism of the formal connection between the Belz court and specific towns across the Galician border (such as Chełm or Wojsławice) — beyond the general testimony of the court's "influence" on nearby Rawa Ruska.

## 4. Examination of the family tradition: Shmuel'ke Schafir and his pilgrimage to the Rebbe of Belz

**Source of the tradition:** according to the written memoirs of Menachem Mendel Schafir (also known as Mendel Schaffer), "Sixty Years of My Life in Wojsławice" (in the Wojsławice yizkor book, Tel Aviv 1970, p. 69), Shmuel "Shmuel'ke" Schafir (born around 1836 in Wojsławice) made the pilgrimage to the Rebbe of Belz [a].

**Findings of the examination:**

- No record, archival source or external historical documentation was found in this search — not in the YIVO Encyclopedia, not on sztetl.org.pl, not in the academic literature on Belz Hasidism and not in the other sources checked — verifying a specific visit by a Jew from Wojsławice or from the Chełm area to the Belz court at the end of the 19th century.
- From the general geographic and historical standpoint **[general background]**: the existence of a regional Hasidic connection between the Chełm area (under Russian control, "Congress Poland") and Hasidic courts in Galicia (under Austro-Hungarian control) — including Belz — is not implausible; indeed, in Wojsławice itself the existence of shtiblekh of the Belz, Ger and Trisk Hasidim was documented [b]. Nevertheless, this is general context only and does not constitute corroboration of the specific visit described in the Schafir memoirs.
- Conclusion: this is a **family tradition only, documented in an internal family source (the Schafir memoirs in the yizkor book)**, without any independent external corroboration found in this search. It should be presented on the family site explicitly as such — not as an externally documented fact.
