# Łódź — The Jewish Community, 1900–1945

> Short research report for the family-history site. A secondary chapter — an appendix to the Albert/Alpert branch (Warsaw–Opatów).
> **Convention:** every documented fact is marked with a source number [n]. Paragraphs marked "general background" are accepted historical context without a specific source citation. Unverified points are explicitly marked as inference.

## 1. The community and the ghetto, in brief

**General background.** Łódź grew in the 19th century from a rural locality into Poland's largest industrial center — especially for the textile industry — and became known as "the Polish Manchester". The Jewish community grew accordingly: about 10,000 Jews in 1870, about 98,000 in 1897, and on the eve of World War II **about 233,000 Jews — 34.7% of the city's residents** — making Łódź the second-largest Jewish community in Poland after Warsaw [1]. Jews owned a substantial share, over half, of the large and medium-sized factories in the textile branch; many others were small merchants, craftsmen, and transport workers [1]. The city had a rich Jewish press in Yiddish ("Lodzer Togblat", "Nayer Folksblat") and in Polish [1].

The German occupation began on 8 September 1939, and from that day the persecutions began: from November 1939 Jews were required to wear a yellow armband, and from December 1939 — a yellow Star of David on the chest and back [1]. In February 1940 the Germans established a ghetto in the northeast of the city; more than 150,000 Jews were moved into the designated area, and the ghetto was sealed in April 1940 [2]. It was **the second-largest ghetto in occupied Poland, after the Warsaw ghetto** [2]. Placed at the head of the Judenrat (Ältestenrat) was **Chaim Mordechai Rumkowski**, who hoped that turning the ghetto into an essential labor force would protect its inhabitants — but he was entirely subject to the Germans' non-negotiable demands [2].

In the spring of 1944 the decision was made to destroy the Łódź ghetto, which was by then the last ghetto remaining in Poland. From 2 August until the end of August 1944, more than 72,000 of the ghetto's inhabitants — Rumkowski himself among them — were deported to Auschwitz-Birkenau; most were murdered on arrival [2]. The Soviet forces that liberated Łódź on 19 January 1945 found in the city **only 870 Jews** who had survived there [2].

## 2. The family point of contact — what was checked in Łódź and what was found

### What the research sought
The research attempted to check whether **Chaim Albert** (the site owner's grandfather) married a Łódź woman in the 1920s or 1930s, before the family's move to Warsaw. To that end, **all the marriage registers (skorowidze) of the Jewish community of Łódź**, 1921–1933, were scanned for the spellings `Albert`, `Alpert`, `Elbert`, `Albirt` [internal source: `metryki.genealodzy.pl`, zespół 1568, State Archive in Łódź].

### The result — negative for Chaim
Across all the years of continuous coverage (1921–1930, and most of 1930–1933), **not a single groom or bride named Albert/Alpert/Albirt/Albert was found**. The JRI-Poland index for Łódź marriages 1936–1939 likewise contains no "Chaim Albert". ⇒ **There is no evidence that Chaim married in Łódź; Warsaw remains the likely destination.**

### What was found — the unrelated "ALBIRT" family from Przedbórz
In scanning the 1932–1933 register, two marriage acts were found of brothers named **Albirt** (Izrael-Wigdor, 1932; Chil-Hilel, 1933), both **born in Przedbórz**, sons of **Szlama Albirt ⚭ Tema née Orbuch**, and official permanent residents of the **gmina of Łopuszno, Kielce district**. Interestingly: the groom Izrael-Wigdor signed the act in his own hand with the spelling **"Albert"**, while the clerk recorded "Albirt" — documentary proof that the spellings were interchangeable within the very same family. ⚠ **There is no proven connection between the Albirt-Przedbórz family and the Albert/Alpert branch of Warsaw–Opatów** (Chaim's parents were David Alpert and Rudla Butkowska of Warsaw, not Szlama and Tema). Przedbórz and Łopuszno do lie in the same Kielce district as Opatów and Iwaniska, the towns of the family's other side — but this is geographic proximity only, not lineage.

### The Łódź survivors list, 1945 — four "Albert"s
In a printed list of survivors registered in Łódź after the liberation (source: Arolsen Archives, *Registration of Liberated Former Persecutees*, Reference DE ITS 3.1.1.3 PL; indexed on Ancestry, dbid 63030) there appears a sequence of four persons named "Albert": Majchen (b. 1907), **Chaim (b. 1913) — this is Chaim Albert himself**, Ranka (b. 1918), and **Szyja (b. 1885, place of birth: Opatow)**. The first three are recorded with a pre-1939 address of "Warsaw" (as a ditto mark, i.e., the same as the record above them). Szyja Albert, born in Opatów in 1885, is an **unproven connection** to the main family branch — no documentation links him to the known lineage, but the combination of the name and the town is striking enough to record him here as an open inference.
