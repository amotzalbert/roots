# Iwaniska and Klimontów — Two Communities in the Opatów Region, 1830–1921

> Research report for the family-history site — the Rywenbajrych family (Rywenbajrych / RUBINBAJRACH), with a focus on **Tuvia Rywenbajrych**, born in Iwaniska in 1888, son of Abe-Leib and Feige-Rachel Rywenbajrych (according to the Opatów register of non-permanent residents, 1921), and on earlier generations of the family documented in Klimontów (marriage 1834, births 1838–1867).
> **Convention:** every documented fact is marked with a source number [n]. Paragraphs marked "general background" are accepted historical context without a specific source citation. Gaps in the sources and unverified points are explicitly marked with **⚠**.

---

## 1. Iwaniska

### Location and administrative status

Iwaniska is a town in the Opatów county (powiat opatowski), Świętokrzyskie Voivodeship, about 14 km southwest of Opatów and about 54 km east of Kielce [1], [4]. In the period under discussion (1830–1921) it was part of the Radom gubernia and later the Kielce gubernia, within Russian-ruled Congress Poland.

### Founding of the community and the 18th century

- According to JewishGen KehilaLinks, Jews apparently settled in Iwaniska in the 18th century; the noble owners of the town, the Zwirowski family, encouraged Jewish settlement as an important factor in economic development and allowed Jews to purchase plots and build houses [3]. ⚠ This detail comes from a search-engine summary of the KehilaLinks page; it has not been verified against the full text (see "source gaps" below).
- Toward the end of the 18th century the religious community in Iwaniska comprised about 12 houses and 15 huts, with a town hall, a hospital, and a school building, averaging about 3.4 persons per house [2].

### The 19th century

- In the mid-19th century Christians were forbidden to serve or work for Jews, following a libel against a woman from Iwaniska who was accused of handing children over to Jews, allegedly for ritual purposes (a blood libel) [2].
- **The November Uprising (1830–1831):** the Jews of Iwaniska gave financial support to the Polish army forces; the religious council donated 20 zlotys to the Sandomierz cavalry [2]. This is the closest documentary reference to the focus period (1830–1921) found in this source.
- **1899:** Rabbi Yitzchak David Shapira was appointed chief rabbi of the community [3].

### The early 20th century and the World War I period

- **1916:** Rabbi Icek Jankiel Widerman (also known as R. Yaakov Yitzchak Widman) began serving as the community's rabbi; his salary ranged around 5,000–5,960 zlotys per year toward the end of the 1920s [2], [3]. He served until the Holocaust.
- **1916:** the first Zionist group in the town was founded [3].

### The interwar period (1918–1939)

- **1924:** the community board included Nachman Zalcman, Shmuel Wajsdorf, Lejbusz Goldman, Emanuel Grynszpan, Lejzor Wetman, Berek Teperman, and Shlomo Czersznia — most of them merchants, mostly religious but politically independent [2].
- **1927:** a "Beit Yaakov" school was founded at the initiative of Agudat Israel; most children studied in a traditional cheder [3].
- **1930:** a Jewish library opened, serving as a meeting place for youth, alongside Hebrew study circles [3].
- **Institutions:** a synagogue, a mikveh, a cheder, a cemetery [2].
- **Population:** according to the records of the starostwo (the district office), 1,450 Jews in 1933, about 2,000 in 1937 [2]; another source cites 1,663 persons on the eve of World War II [3]. ⚠ There is a discrepancy between the sources (1937: ~2,000 vs. "on the eve of the war": 1,663) — this may reflect different counting methods (registered religious community vs. actual residents) or a population decline between 1937 and 1939; no explicit explanation was found in the sources.
- **Economy:** the main branches were milling and tanning (leather processing); merchants dealt in cattle, dairy products, hides, textiles, and food; craftsmen — tailors, shoemakers, hatters, and butchers; a weekly market for the sale of horses, cattle, and produce [2].
- **Economic hardship:** the community budget suffered from persistent deficits; arrears in the collection of membership dues reached 3,070 zlotys in 1927 [2].
- **The Pinkas Hakehillot encyclopaedia:** *Pinkas Hakehillot Polin*, vol. VII (Lublin and Kielce districts, Yad Vashem, 1999) includes a separate entry on Iwaniska on pp. 70–72 [5]. ⚠ We were unable to locate/retrieve the full text of the entry on the JewishGen site (the specific URL was not found); check directly at jewishgen.org/Yizkor/pinkas_poland/pinkas_poland7.html or in a printed copy.

### World War II (a brief aside, outside the focus period)

The German occupation began in September 1939; in 1942 all the Jews of Iwaniska were deported to Treblinka [3].

### ⚠ The Jewish registers of Iwaniska — did not survive / not known to be available

This is a central and important finding for the family research: according to JRI-Poland (the largest indexing project for Jewish records from Poland, over 6 million records from 550+ towns), **no Jewish civil-registration records exist (indexed) for Iwaniska itself**. However, the marriage extracts of **Opatów** include **18 grooms/brides from Iwaniska** — that is, Jews from Iwaniska who married were recorded in the Opatów registers [6]. This fact is consistent with the structure of the regional relationship described in section 3 below: Iwaniska appears to have been administratively and registrationally tied to Opatów, at least for some family events. **We have not guessed at or invented the contents** of the Iwaniska registers themselves — we have no confirmation that they survived at all in the state archive, and no contradicting information was found. This should be checked directly with the Archiwum Państwowe w Kielcach (Sandomierz branch) and with the catalog of the local USC (Urząd Stanu Cywilnego).

## 2. Klimontów

### Founding and status

Klimontów is an ancient settlement — habitation is documented as early as the 12th century, on the Pokrzewka river [7]. Town status (praw miejskich) was granted in **1604**, when the nobleman Jan Zbigniew Ossoliński received a royal privilege from King Zygmunt III [7]. Important for our purposes: the charter **did not include** the restrictive clause *"de non tolerandis Judaeis"* (a ban on Jewish settlement) — on the contrary, the privilege declared that "the town is intended for free and honest people of all crafts and professions, without distinction of religion" [7]. A further privilege from **1611** granted market-day and fair rights, which enabled economic growth [8].

### The 17th century — the Swedish depredations

In the Swedish invasion of Poland (1655–1656, "the Deluge") the Jews of Klimontów suffered together with the rest of the inhabitants; and after the town was liberated by Polish soldiers, the Jews were attacked by them as well [8]. The documentation of this season of persecution is among the few records preserved about the community in the 17th century.

### Founding of the kahal and its status relative to Opatów

At the beginning of the 18th century the Jews of Klimontów founded a **community (kahal)**, which was **subordinate to the larger kahal in nearby Opatów** [7], [8]. This is a key point for identifying the relationship between the two towns and Opatów — see section 3.

### Livelihoods and economy

- Most residents made their living from agriculture and trade, alongside craftsmen [7].
- In the 18th century a weaving and textile industry developed in the town [7].
- Until the end of the 19th century Jews operated most of the town's shops as well as five taverns/inns (1827), plus two soda-water plants and mills [8].
- Common occupations: peddlers, day laborers, dairy farmers, tailors, and shoemakers serving the peasants of the surrounding area [7], [8].

### Institutions

- A wooden synagogue and a cemetery were established as early as the 17th century [7].
- A new stone synagogue, at a cost of 30,000 zlotys, was completed in **1851**, alongside the rabbi's house, a mikveh, a hospital, and two cheders [7], [8].

### The interwar period (1918–1939)

- After World War I the Jewish population grew significantly; two batei midrash and additional informal minyanim were established [7].
- Social institutions multiplied: charity funds, hospitality/hospice services, and health insurance (a sick fund) [7].
- A branch of the Zionist Organization and Zionist parties such as "HaMizrachi" were active [7]; a modern Hebrew school and a Zionist library operated [7].
- In the 1920s–30s antisemitism intensified — discriminatory policies toward Jewish businesses and ongoing harassment by the authorities and police [7].

### Civil registration — status

Civil registration in Klimontów began in **1808**; the kahal recorded births, marriages, and deaths from **1826** onward [8]. This is the documentary explanation for why the history of the Rywenbajrych family can be traced in Klimontów as early as the 1830s (marriage 1834) and throughout 1838–1867 — the community kept continuous registers throughout the entire period under study, unlike Iwaniska.

### World War II (a brief aside, outside the focus period)

A ghetto was established in Klimontów during the German occupation; most of the Jewish community was exterminated [9], [10].

## 3. The relationship between the two towns

### Distance and geographic proximity

The travel distance between Iwaniska and Klimontów is about **16.6 km** [11]. The gmina (local authority) of Iwaniska borders the gmina of Klimontów to the east [12]. The two towns lie in **adjacent counties**: Iwaniska in powiat opatowski, together with Opatów itself about 14 km away [1], [4], and Klimontów in **powiat sandomierski** — which is also why its registers are held at the Sandomierz branch of the state archive. The three towns form a tight geographic triangle (a radius of about 15–20 km), typical of the dense network of Jewish settlement in the Kielce–Radom region.

### Shared communal subordination to Opatów

This is the most significant documented point of contact: **the Klimontów community was founded in the 18th century in formal subordination to the large kahal of Opatów** [7], [8]. In parallel, JRI-Poland data show that marriage records of the Jews of **Iwaniska** are found within the registers of **Opatów** (18 grooms/brides) and not in independent registers of Iwaniska [6]. Combining these two findings — Klimontów's subordination to Opatów on the one hand, and the registration of Iwaniska marriages in Opatów on the other — suggests a **consistent pattern**: Opatów served as a registration/supra-communal center for the cluster of small and medium-sized communities around it, including Klimontów and Iwaniska. ⚠ This is a reasonable reconstruction based on two independent sources, but no source was found stating it explicitly as a unified official policy for all three towns together — it should be treated as a well-grounded working hypothesis, not as a fact proven to the end.

### A regional trade network

A source on Opatów (the yizkor book "Apt", 1966) documents that the Jewish grain merchants of Opatów also operated on the market days of the neighboring towns — **among them Klimontów and Iwaniska explicitly**, together with Ostrowiec, Ćmielów, Ożarów, and Łagów [13]. Thus the connection between the three towns was not merely administrative-communal but also a living commercial-economic one: merchant families moved among the market days and fairs of the various towns.

### Conclusion regarding the family's movement

The combination of (a) geographic proximity of 14–17 km between the three towns, (b) the communal-registrational subordination of Klimontów, and of marriage events from Iwaniska, to Opatów, and (c) a shared regional trade network — provides a plausible historical explanation for the movement of the Rywenbajrych family: earlier generations are documented in Klimontów (1834–1867), apparently as part of the community subordinate to Opatów; the family branch of Abe-Leib and Feige-Rachel is found in Iwaniska, where Tuvia was born in 1888; and finally the family is documented in the register of non-permanent residents of Opatów itself in 1921 — that is, "returning" to the communal-commercial center to which it had been tied all along. ⚠ This is a plausible historical reconstruction (general background), not a directly documented fact — we possess no document linking the family's generations in Klimontów to the branch in Iwaniska by name and date.
