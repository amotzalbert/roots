## 1. Chronological Overview

### 1.1 From Antiquity to the Early 19th Century — Brief Background

Volodymyr (known to the Jews as Ludmir — לודמיר; in Russian and in the archival documentation of the period under study: Vladimir-Volynsky, Владимир-Волынский; in Polish: Włodzimierz Wołyński) is one of the oldest cities of Volhynia, and formerly the capital of the Principality of Volhynia. The first mentions of Jews in the city date to 1171 [1][2]; according to a local researcher, Jews may have reached the region as early as the 10th century in the wake of trade [2]. The community developed as a merchant community whose connections reached as far as the Rhine [2]. After Volhynia was annexed to Poland in 1569 the community grew stronger, and its rabbis and communal leaders represented Volhynia in the Council of Four Lands [1][2]. The community was severely harmed in the Khmelnytsky massacres (1648–1649), and after the capture of the city by Lithuanian forces in 1653 only two Jewish families remained in it [3]. **[General background]** A well-known figure from a later period is Hannah Rachel Verbermacher (1806–1892), "the Maiden of Ludmir," a charismatic Hasidic leader who was active in the city [3].

### 1.2 The 19th Century: Recovery and Demographic Growth

In the 18th–19th centuries the city grew rapidly thanks to extensive Jewish settlement, within the framework of the Russian Pale of Settlement [1][4]. According to the "Geographical Dictionary of the Kingdom of Poland" (a Polish source from the end of the 19th century), the city at that time numbered 8,336 residents, of whom 6,122 were Jews — about 73% [4]. **The community then constituted an absolute majority of the city's population.**

| Year | Total residents | Jews | Percentage of Jews |

| 1653 | — | 2 families | — |
| Late 19th century (~1880) | 8,336 | 6,122 | ~73% |
| 1910–1911 | 15,622 | 7,060 | ~45% |
| 1921 (independent Poland) | — | — | 48.2%* |

*1921 figure: 48.2% Jews, 32.0% Poles, 16.8% Ukrainians, 2.6% Russians [4][5].

A comparison of the two censuses (late 19th century versus 1910–1911) shows that the city's population nearly doubled within about three decades (from 8,336 to 15,622), while the relative Jewish percentage declined (from 73% to about 45%) — a sign of significant non-Jewish migration into the city alongside the economic growth (see 1.3), and perhaps also of Jewish emigration out of the city in the same period.

### 1.3 The Late 19th Century: The Railway and Industry

The city's railway station opened in 1908 [4], an event that joins the broad trend of the development of the Russian railway network at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th centuries, which linked the cities of Volhynia to the centers of commerce. **[General background]** Across the Pale of Settlement, Jewish capital played a significant role in financing private railway lines in that period, and the opening of railway stations generally accelerated local trade and the migration of the rural population to the market towns — a phenomenon consistent with the population jump documented between the late 19th century and 1910–1911 (Section 1.2). However, no specific source has been located describing the railway's impact on the economy of the Jewish community in Volodymyr-Volynskyi itself, and therefore the link between the two remains a reasonable inference and not a documented fact.

### 1.4 Livelihoods in the 19th and Early 20th Centuries

The main Jewish livelihood in the city was based on trade in cattle — and especially horses — and in grain [4]. This is a typical pattern for market towns in Volhynia bordering extensive agricultural areas, in which the Jewish merchants served as intermediaries between village produce and the urban markets and inter-regional trade.

### 1.5 Institutions and Public Life

In the 1920s and 1930s about 20 active synagogues operated in the city [4] — a number attesting to a large and organized community that developed over the course of the 19th century. The main synagogue was built, as far as is known, in 1801, and survived World War II but was demolished in the early 1950s [6]. At the end of the 19th century a branch of the "Hibbat Zion" movement was founded in the city, and from 1906 a branch of the "Bund" — the Marxist Jewish proletarian party — was active there as well [4]. This activity attests to a community with vibrant ideological and party life already in the years before World War I, both in the Zionist direction and in the Jewish-socialist direction.

Alongside these, the community's memorial (yizkor) book ("Pinkas Ludmir" — פנקס לודמיר, Tel Aviv 1962, about 624 columns) documented further details on institutions, personalities, and families that had not yet been fully processed and translated into English or Hebrew as of the writing of this report [7][8].

## 2. Community Life — Summary for the Period 1840–1914

- **Economy:** trade in cattle, horses, and grain as the leading sector; most likely also crafts and retail trade, as was customary in the towns of Volhynia, but without specific detail in the sources surveyed for this report [4].
- **Demography:** a large community that constituted a majority of the city's population throughout most of the period (from 73% around 1880 to 45% in 1910–1911, a relative decline against the background of overall urban growth) [4].
- **Religious institutions:** about 20 synagogues and batei midrash in the city in the subsequent period (the 1920s–30s, as an indication of the number of houses of prayer that developed over the 19th century) [4]; a main synagogue from 1801 [6].
- **Movements and ideology:** "Hibbat Zion" (late 19th century) and the "Bund" (from 1906) [4] — a sign of the modernization and politicization of the community, similar to other large Jewish communities in the Pale of Settlement in those years.
- **Infrastructure:** a railway station (1908) [4], connecting the city to the regional and national trade network.

## 3. The Turbulent Period: World War I and the Russian Revolution, 1914–1920

### 3.1 1914–1915: The Outbreak of War and the Front

Volodymyr-Volynskyi lay close to the Austro-Russian border, and in the summer of 1915 — after the fall of Lwów to the Austro-Hungarian–German army in June 1915 — the Eastern Front line stabilized along the axis Chełm–Volodymyr-Volynskyi–Brody–Buchach [9]. The city was captured by Austrian forces in 1915 [4][9]. **[General background]** Across Volhynia and Lithuania, the retreat of the Russian army in the spring of 1915 was accompanied by the mass expulsion of Jews from the front-line areas on charges of wholesale "treason" — about 200,000 Jews were expelled from the Kovno and Courland provinces in May 1915 alone [9]. No source has been located specifying whether the community of Volodymyr-Volynskyi itself underwent a similar organized expulsion, but its location on the front line places it at the heart of a region that suffered severely from expulsion, forced migration, and fighting in those years.

### 3.2 1915–1918: Occupation and Changes of Rule

Under the Austrian occupation, the first Ukrainian school in the city was established in 1916 by soldiers of the "Ukrainian Sich," and toward the end of the war, in 1918, the city served as a staging point for a German division (the "Greycoat Division") [4]. This period of changing rulers (Russian → Austrian → German) reflected the instability that characterized the entire Eastern Front region.

### 3.3 1918–1920: The Civil War and the Clash of Regimes

With the collapse of the Russian Empire and the end of the World War, Volhynia became a battleground between Ukrainian, Bolshevik, and Polish forces. Already in the summer of 1917 the murder and robbery of Jews by soldiers retreating from the front were documented in various parts of Volhynia [9]; the situation deteriorated with the outbreak of fighting between Ukrainians and Bolsheviks in 1918, and reached its peak in a wave of pogroms carried out by armed bands in 1919–1920, which claimed many thousands of lives [9].

In Volodymyr-Volynskyi itself: after battles between Ukrainian and Polish forces, the Polish army captured the city on 23 January 1919 [4]. In the summer of 1919 a wave of pogroms swept the communities of Volhynia and Podolia at the hands of Petliura's armies, in which about 150,000 Jews perished in the region as a whole (a general estimate, not specific to the city) [10]. In the Polish–Soviet War of 1920 the Jews of Volhynia suffered at the hands of both sides; the First Cavalry Army under Semyon Budyonny — whose soldiers were mostly Cossacks from the Kuban, among whom traditional antisemitism was widespread — carried out some of the worst pogroms of the Red Army during its retreat from Poland. Between 1 and 6 September 1920 the First Army under Budyonny's command was encamped in the Bug River area, in the Volodymyr-Volynskyi district, at the height of that retreat [11]. The writer Isaac Babel, who accompanied the army as a war correspondent, later documented these pogroms in his work "Red Cavalry" [11]. No source has been located pointing explicitly to a pogrom that took place inside the city of Volodymyr-Volynskyi itself in those days; the link between the army's presence in the district area and the fate of the community in the city remains a matter of geographic plausibility and not a directly documented fact.

### 3.4 The Impact on Jewish Emigration

**[General background]** The trend of Jewish emigration from Volhynia and from similar regions of Eastern Europe grew significantly in the decade preceding World War I (poverty, decrees, the pogroms of the early 20th century) and continued and even intensified in its wake: about 400,000 Jews from Eastern Europe emigrated to the United States and the Land of Israel between 1919 and 1925 [12], against the background of a combination of economic devastation, the trauma of the war, and the pogroms of 1918–1920. The community of Volodymyr-Volynskyi, which stood at the heart of the front-line region and afterward at the heart of the pogrom region, fits this broad pattern, even though no emigration figures specific to the city itself were located in the sources surveyed.
