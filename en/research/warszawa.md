## 1. Chronology: The Largest Jewish Community in Europe

### 1860–1914: Unprecedented Growth

**[Background]** In the second half of the 19th century Warsaw became the largest Jewish community in Europe. The abolition of residence restrictions (the "Jewish quarters" of the Prussian era) in 1862, industrialization, and the railways that turned Warsaw into the commercial junction of "Congress Poland" — drew tens of thousands of Jews from the provincial towns. Accepted estimates: about 72,000 Jews in 1864, about 220,000 in 1897 (the Russian Empire census), and about 337,000 on the eve of World War I — a third or more of the city's residents. Only New York surpassed it in the entire world.

**[Background]** This internal migration is precisely the family story: families like Alpert (Warsaw) who married daughters of small towns like Piątek (near Łęczyca) — a very common pattern in the generation of the 1890s.

**[Background]** The community was polarized and diverse: Hasidism (Ger, Aleksander) alongside maskilim and assimilationists; a network of cheders and shtiblekh alongside gymnasiums; and at the beginning of the 20th century — a flourishing Yiddish press ("Hatzefira" in Hebrew, "Haynt" and "Moment" in Yiddish), Yiddish theater, and political parties: the Bund, Zionists, Agudat Israel.

### 1914–1918: World War I

**[Background]** In August 1915 the Russians retreated and the Germans captured Warsaw. The German occupation brought hunger and epidemics, but also political and cultural liberalization — and it was what changed the population-registration system (see Section 3).

### 1918–1939: Between the Wars

**[Background]** In independent Poland the Jewry of Warsaw numbered about 310,000 in the 1931 census (about 30% of the city) and about 350,000–380,000 on the eve of the war. This was a period of cultural ferment — press, literature (I. L. Peretz died in 1915; an entire generation continued in his footsteps), education networks (CISZO, Tarbut, Beit Yaakov) — alongside economic deterioration in the 1930s, an antisemitic economic boycott, and growing violence.

### September 1939: The Siege

**[Background]** Warsaw was bombed from the very first day of the war, 1.9.1939, and surrendered on 28.9.1939. The northern Jewish quarter was hit especially hard in the bombings, above all on "Black Monday" (25.9.1939). **[Source: Polish Wikipedia, entry ulica Leszno w Warszawie]** Leszno Street itself was damaged in the defense of Warsaw in September 1939. Whoever lived at Leszno 36 in those weeks — like Chaim Albert — experienced the siege at the epicenter of the bombings.

### 1940–1942: The Ghetto

**[Source: Polish Wikipedia, entry ulica Leszno w Warszawie]** In November 1940 Leszno Street — up to Żelazna Street — was enclosed within the Warsaw Ghetto. **[Background]** Some 400,000 and even more (with the deportees from the provincial towns) were crammed into the ghetto on less than 2.5% of the city's area. Hunger, typhus, and overcrowding killed some 80,000–100,000 people even before the deportations.

### Summer 1942: The Great Deportation

**[Background]** Between 22.7.1942 and 21.9.1942 about 265,000 of the ghetto's Jews were deported to the Treblinka extermination camp. After the "Great Aktion" about 60,000 remained in the ghetto, most of them workers of the "shops" (German workshops) — among them the Toebbens workshops, some of which operated on Leszno Street.

### April–May 1943: The Uprising and the Destruction

**[Background]** On 19.4.1943 the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising broke out; on 16.5.1943 Jürgen Stroop blew up the Great Synagogue on Tłomackie (near the eastern end of Leszno) as a sign of "victory." The entire ghetto — and Muranów within it — was destroyed to its foundations and leveled. After the war a new residential district was built on the ruins; the street layout was changed, and most of the historical addresses — including Leszno 36 — no longer exist. **[Source: Polish Wikipedia]** In 1949 the eastern-central part of Leszno was incorporated into the W-Z axis and its name was changed (today Aleja "Solidarności"); only the western section retains the name "Leszno."

## 2. Muranów and Leszno Up Close

### Muranów — the Northern Jewish Quarter

**[Background]** "Muranów" (named after the 17th-century Murano palace) was the heart of the northern-western Jewish quarter: Nalewki, Gęsia, Miła, Nowolipie, Nowolipki, Smocza, Pawia, Dzielna — and Leszno on its southern edge. At the end of the 19th century these streets were 80%–90% Jewish and more. The density was among the highest in Europe: apartment buildings ("kamienice") of 4–6 stories around inner courtyards, sometimes with a second and third courtyard behind them, with dozens of families, workshops, and shtiblekh in each courtyard.

**[Background]** The typical livelihoods: tailoring, shoemaking, furriery, leather processing, weaving and textiles, tinsmithing and carpentry, peddling and petty trade. Production was mostly done in home workshops — the room served as dwelling, workshop, and shop at once. Nalewki Street was the wholesale-trade artery; Gęsia — the fabric trade; and Leszno — a more "respectable," mixed street, with commerce, institutions, and a petite bourgeoisie.

**[Background]** The family's points-of-contact streets: **Smocza** — a poor, crowded street in the heart of Muranów, almost entirely Jewish, where one of the wedding witnesses lived in 1894; **Ostrowska** — a small street at the northern end of the quarter, near Stawki, among the poorest in the city (commemorated in Henry Orenstein's book and in survivors' memoirs as a street of severe destitution), where the second witness lived. Both streets were swallowed into the ghetto and erased in 1943.

### Leszno Street

**[Source: Polish Wikipedia]** The street grew out of the Warsaw–Błonie trade road, and its origin lies in the "jurydyka" (private town) of Leszno founded on 8.1.1648 by Bogusław and Jan Leszczyński; at first it attracted Protestant settlers (hence the Calvinist church in it). **[Background]** In the 19th century it became a main artery in the west of the city, and with the spread of the Jewish population southward from Muranów — a mostly Jewish street: in 1938 about 80% of its residents were Jews.

**[Background]** What was on the street:
- **Houses of prayer and religious institutions** — dozens of shtiblekh and batei midrash in the courtyards; at its eastern end, in nearby Tłomackie Square, stood the Great Synagogue (1878) and the Main Judaic Library (today the ŻIH institute).
- **The courthouses** — the "Gmach Sądów" (Gmach Sądów na Lesznie), the monumental courthouse building at Leszno 53/55, built 1935–1939 to a design by Bohdan Pniewski. **[Source: Polish Wikipedia]** During the occupation the entire street was called Gerichtstraße ("Court Street") after it. **[Background]** In the ghetto years the building stood on the seam line — entrance from Leszno (in the ghetto) and exit to the "Aryan" side — and served as a well-known smuggling and escape route.
- **Street life** — a tram ran along it; cafés, a cinema and theater, shops; one of the lively streets of Jewish Warsaw.

### Leszno Inside the Ghetto: The "Boulevard" of the Imprisoned City

**[Background]** Within the ghetto, Leszno became one of the two main arteries of life (together with Karmelicka Street), and in memoirs of the period it was often called "the Broadway of the ghetto": the "Femina" theater operated there (Leszno 35), Café "Sztuka" (Leszno 2), the most famous of the ghetto's cafés, where the pianist Władysław Szpilman and the singer Marysia Ajzensztadt ("the Nightingale of the Ghetto") performed; and at Leszno 13 sat the infamous office of Abraham Gancwajch (the "Office to Combat Usury and Profiteering," "the Thirteen"), which collaborated with the Gestapo. A central ghetto gate stood at the corner of Leszno–Żelazna (documented in a photograph, see Section 6). In August 1942, during the Great Deportation, the ghetto was reduced and Leszno gradually emptied; its houses were burned and destroyed in the 1943 uprising and afterward.

### The Leszno 30–40 Section — the Immediate Surroundings of Leszno 36

- **[Source: warszawa1939.pl, obiekt/leszno-36]** On the building at Leszno 36 itself, the "Warsaw 1939" foundation has no architectural information (owners, architect, year of construction) — but its archive holds **a photograph of the house from before 1915** (donated by Andrzej Żmirek), and the building is marked "does not exist" (OBIEKT NIE ISTNIEJE). This is a practical research direction: contact the foundation and request the photograph.
- **[Source: warszawa1939.pl, widok/leszno-d]** Plot 36 is described in the foundation's street photographs as neighboring the **Carmelite church and monastery complex** (Leszno 32/34, the Church of the Nativity of the Blessed Virgin Mary) — meaning, Chaim Albert lived right next to the most prominent landmark in this section. **[Background]** The church survived the war, served the converted Jews in the ghetto ("the Catholics of Jewish race"), and in 1962 the whole church was moved about 21 meters northward during the widening of the axis — one of Warsaw's famous engineering operations. It stands to this day (Al. Solidarności 80) — **the closest site to Leszno 36 that can be visited today**.
- **[Source: warszawa1939.pl, obiekt/leszno-35]** Opposite, at Leszno 35, stood the modern building in which the "Femina" cinema/theater opened — in the ghetto it was a central theater, and today it is one of the few buildings that survived in this section.
- **[Source: warszawa1939.pl, obiekt/leszno-25]** At Leszno 25 stood the Jacobson kamienica — an example of the street's bourgeois apartment houses.
- **[Background]** The 30–40 section was thus a typical mixed section: crowded Jewish apartment houses on both sides of an old church complex — a distinctly Warsaw picture.

## 3. The Registration System: Cyrkuł and Komisariat

### Until 1915: Registration by Cyrkuł

**[Source: JRI-Poland, "Warszawa Registration by Cyrkul/Districts"]** Warsaw was divided into "cyrkuły" — police-administrative districts whose number and boundaries changed over the period (1870–1916 and onward), and **each individual cyrkuł kept its own civil-status books (births, marriages, deaths)**; in certain periods the registration of several cyrkuły was combined.

**[Background]** This is how it worked in practice: from 1826 the Jews in "Congress Poland" were required to have separate civil registration. In Warsaw, a civil-status registrar for Jewish records was appointed in each cyrkuł, and the event was registered in the cyrkuł according to the **place of residence/event** — not according to community. The registration was kept in Polish (until 1867/8) and afterward in Russian, in the Napoleonic narrative format: date, names of the parents/spouses, their ages, occupations, address — and **two witnesses by name, age, occupation, and address**. Hence the enormous genealogical value: act 75 of Cyrkuł V of 18.2.1894 (the marriage of Dawid Alpert and Rudla) gives us not only the couple and their parents but also witnesses residing on Ostrowska and Smocza — a geographic anchor placing the family's circle in the heart of Muranów.

**[Background]** Cyrkuł V extended in this period over part of the northern-western Jewish quarter (the Nowolipki–Dzielna–Smocza area, according to the JRI-Poland maps based on Bartoszewski–Polonsky, *The Jews in Warsaw*, 1991). That is: the wedding was registered in the cyrkuł of the quarter where the family lived. *(The exact boundaries for 1894 should be verified against the JRI-Poland map — the boundaries shifted.)*

### After 1915: Registration by Komisariat

**[Background]** With the German occupation in 1915 and the transition to Polish independence, the Russian cyrkuł system was abolished. The civil-status records of Warsaw's Jews were kept from then on according to **police komisariats** (Komisariat Policji) — the numbered municipal police stations, each of which served a defined area. The principle remained the same: registration by residential address, and the komisariat number is a geographic key. **The fact that Chaim Albert appears in September 1939 in Komisariat III at the address Leszno 36** is consistent with Komisariat III serving this area of the west of the city center. *(Precise mapping of the boundaries of Komisariat III in 1939 — a follow-up task; it can be verified against the Warsaw address books and against JRI-Poland.)*

**[Background]** Where the records are today: the surviving cyrkuł books are held in the State Archive in Warsaw (and some in AGAD), and computerized indexes of them — at JRI-Poland (about 17,000 Warsaw records 1869–1898 and more) and at Geneteka. A large portion of the Warsaw books was lost in the war — and therefore every surviving act, like 75/1894, is an asset.
