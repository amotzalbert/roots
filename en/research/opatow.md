# Opatów (אַפְּטָא) — the Jewish community, 1780–1942

> Research report for the family-history site — the Rywenbajrych, Wajsblum, Ginsburg and Lubliner families.
> **Convention:** every documented fact is marked with a source number [n]. Paragraphs marked "general background" are accepted historical context without a specific source citation. Points that have not been verified are marked explicitly.

---

## 1. Chronology

### Before 1780 — the community's roots

- **1538–1545:** the beginning of documented Jewish settlement. The original charter of privileges for the Jews of Opatów was granted in 1545 by the Grand Hetman Jan Amor Tarnowski; Opatów is considered the first town in the Sandomierz Voivodeship in which Jews settled [1], [2].
- **1634:** the town was officially divided into two parts — Christian and Jewish; the Jewish part was called the "Jewish town," and at its center ran the "Jewish street" (Ulica Żydowska; in Yiddish "Yidishe gas" — יידישע גאס, as well as "Breyte gas" — ברייטע גאס and "Shmale gas" — שמאלע גאס) [1], [6].
- **17th century:** the Great Synagogue was built of stone, on a plot between the town wall and the central axis of the Jewish town; a Gothic structure with four massive corner pillars and wall paintings of the zodiac wheel [1], [6], [12].
- **1714:** the Jewish quarter, densely built of wood, burned down completely in one of the many fires that struck it [1].
- **The community's standing:** Opatów was the seat of one of the six districts of the Sandomierz–Kraków "Lands of the Jews" within the framework of the Council of Four Lands; in 1754 the Opatów district included 13 additional communities, among them Sandomierz, Baranów and Dzików [1]. This is the basis for the assertion that Opatów was among the important communities of Lesser Poland (Małopolska).

### The 18th century — according to Gershon Hundert's research

The book **Gershon David Hundert, *The Jews in a Polish Private Town: The Case of Opatów in the Eighteenth Century*** (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1992) is the comprehensive academic study of the community [8]:

- Opatów was a **private town** owned by magnate families (high nobility), not a royal town — a situation that characterized most of Polish Jewry: in the 18th century more than half of the world's Jews lived in the private towns and villages of Polish magnates, and roughly half of Poland's total urban population was Jewish [8].
- Hundert documents a community of **about 2,000 Jews in the 1765 census** [8], covering the structure of the kahal, the community's relations with the town's owners, the taxes, the internal disputes and the economy.
- **1760:** King Augustus III appointed Pejsak Chaimowicz of Opatów as a royal Jewish factor (agent) [2].

### 1780–1914 — from the partitions to the Russian Empire

- **1795:** in the Third Partition of Poland, Opatów was annexed to Austria; **1809** — to the Duchy of Warsaw; **1815** — to Congress Poland under Russian rule [2].
- **1842:** Opatów became a district (powiat) town [2].
- **c. 1809–1813:** R. Avraham Yehoshua Heschel, "the Rebbe of Apta," served in the town (see chapter 3; the sources disagree on the exact years) [9], [10].
- **1856:** the town had 2,517 Jews out of 3,845 inhabitants — about two thirds of the town [2] (the Polish source cites approximately 2,510 [1]).
- **1897:** 4,138 Jews [2].
- **1915:** Austrian occupation in World War I; **1918** — Polish independence [2].

### Between the wars (1918–1939)

- In the early 1920s about 5,500 Jews lived in Opatów — roughly two thirds of the population [1], [4].
- The community maintained two banks, charitable institutions, a hospital and educational networks of all the movements [4].
- Active in the town were: the Zionist Organization (headed by **Mordechai Wajsblum** — see chapter 4 — and Michael Rothstein), which founded "Tarbut" Hebrew-language schools; "HaMizrachi" (the "Torah VaDaat — Yavne" school); Agudat Yisrael ("Yesodei HaTorah" and "Beit Yaakov"); Poalei Zion, the Bund and communist circles; Betar; and associations of merchants and artisans [7].
- Hasidic shtiblekh of Ger, Ostrowiec, Modzhitz, Aleksander and Belz operated alongside the Great Synagogue and four batei midrash [7].
- The town produced the folk painter Mayer Kirshenblatt, whose paintings of the "Apt" of the 1920s were exhibited at the POLIN Museum in Warsaw [2], [13].

### The Holocaust (1939–1942)

- **September 1939:** the German occupation. The Germans set fire to the market area and imprisoned 1,000–1,500 residents (Poles and Jews) in the cinema building for two days without food; about 200 young Jews were abducted and did not return [4].
- **Early 1940:** the obligation to wear a white armband with a Star of David, restrictions on movement and commerce; a Judenrat was established headed by **M. Weisblum**, whose office was at 8 Nowo-Wałowa Street; the Judenrat was repeatedly extorted with ransom demands [4].
- **April 1941** (April 6 according to the Polish source): an **open ghetto** was established in the area of the streets **Joselewicza, Zatylna, Wąska and Starowałowa** [2], [4], [5]. Its population reached about 7,000 and at its peak up to about 10,000 souls, including refugees and deportees from Warsaw, Łódź and Vienna [2], [4], [5].
- In the ghetto: severe overcrowding and typhus epidemics; a TOZ clinic, a hospital of 30–40 beds, two public kitchens; on July 16, 1941 a school for 220 children opened, in Hebrew and Yiddish; the Judenrat operated a brush-making workshop; the Dror and Hashomer Hatzair movements ran training farms led by Chaim Czerniakowski [4].
- **Underground:** young people organized an underground movement and obtained weapons through Polish contacts in Ostrowiec; in a Gestapo raid in 1942 the arms cache was confiscated and female members in hiding as well as passers-by were murdered [4].
- **Liquidation of the ghetto, October 20–22, 1942 (Operation Reinhard):** German gendarmerie, SS men and Ukrainian auxiliaries ("Trawnikis") surrounded the ghetto. About 6,500 men, women and children were assembled in Targowica Square and marched about 18 km to the Jasice railway station; the weak at the rear of the column were beaten and shot. About 120 people were crammed into each car; the journey to Treblinka took about three days without food or water, and most of the deportees were murdered in the gas chambers upon arrival [4], [5]. The total number deported to Treblinka is estimated at about 7,000 [4] to 8,000 [1], [2]; about 2,000 were sent to labor camps — Sandomierz, Starachowice, Radom and Skarżysko-Kamienna (HASAG) [2], [5]. Those kept behind to clear the ghetto were ultimately murdered at the Jewish cemetery [4].
- The yizkor book counts **only about 300 survivors** from a community of about 8,000 souls [7]. Among them was Samuel Willenberg, who was deported from Opatów to Treblinka on October 20, 1942, escaped in the Treblinka revolt and testified about the deportation [3], [4].
- The 17th-century synagogue was destroyed by the Germans during the war [12].
- The community's memorial (yizkor) book: **"Apt: Sefer zikaron le-ir va-em be-Yisrael — a town which is no more" (Apt; A Town Which Does Not Exist Anymore)**, edited by Z. Yasheev, published by the Apt Organization in Israel, USA, Canada and Brazil, Tel Aviv 1966; Hebrew, Yiddish and English [6], [7].

## 2. Daily life and livelihoods

**Documented:**

- The Jewish economy rested on petty trade, crafts and transport services [2]. On the eve of World War II the Jews of Opatów engaged mainly in commerce and small industry — **clothing, food and tanning (leather processing)** [4].
- The prominent artisans: tailors, shoemakers and carpenters. Among the merchants noted in the yizkor book: Eliezer Mendelbaum, owner of a factory for soap, candles and chemicals; Binyamin Loewenstein; and Henoch Langer [7].
- **The market:** the market square (rynek) was the heart of livelihood; the Jewish grain merchants also worked the market days of the neighboring towns — Ostrowiec, Ćmielów, Ożarów, Klimontów, Iwaniska and Łagów [7].
- The town sat on the Opatówka river, which encircled it; the main trade roads led toward Iwaniska, Ostrowiec, Sandomierz and Ożarów, and an iron bridge crossed the river on the way to the Ostrowiec railway station [7].

**General background:** this pattern of livelihoods — a shop on the market square, a stall on fair day, a craft at home, and trade in grain and hides with the surrounding villages — characterized the entire Kielce-Radom region's small towns. A Jewish merchant in the Opatów of the 1920s, like **Tuvia Rywenbajrych**, operated within a regional network of market and fair days that linked Opatów to Ostrowiec and Sandomierz.

### Drewska Street — what was found and what was not

- **Not located** online (Wirtualny Sztetl, Polish Wikipedia, current maps, local history sites): any explicit mention of a street named Drewska in Opatów. Its name does not appear in the list of ghetto streets (1941) and does not exist on the current town map [1], [2], [11].
- **Cautious conclusions (not a documented fact):** the name Drewska most likely derives from drewno ("wood") — a common pattern for streets of timber trade or wooden construction in Polish towns; the street may have changed its name between the 1921 register and the present, as happened to many streets in Poland in the 1920s–30s. **The family document (the 1921 residents' register) is currently the only evidence we have of the street's name** — and that in itself is an archival finding of value.
- **Recommendation for follow-up:** check old town plans of Opatów at the State Archive in Kielce (Archiwum Państwowe w Kielcach, Sandomierz branch), and the Polish address books (Księgi adresowe) of the 1920s, which contain full street listings.

## 3. Hasidism in Apta — R. Avraham Yehoshua Heschel, the "Ohev Yisrael"

**Documented:**

- R. Avraham Yehoshua Heschel was born around 5508 (1748); he was a disciple of R. Elimelech of Lizhensk and R. Yechiel Michel of Zlotchov, and was counted among the four "heirs" of R. Elimelech [9], [10].
- He served as rabbi in Kolbuszowa, and afterwards **in Apta (Opatów)** — according to one source from about 1800 [10], according to another in the years 1809–1813 [9] — and later in Iași in Moldavia; in 1813–1814 he settled in Medzhybizh in Podolia, where he lived until his death on 5 Nisan 5585 (1825). He was buried in the old cemetery plot in Medzhybizh, near the grave of the Baal Shem Tov [9], [10].
- Although he served in many communities, among the Hasidim he always remained **"der Apter Rov" (דער אַפּטער רב)** — the Rebbe of Apta; it was the town that gave him his name for the generations [9].
- He instructed that only **"Ohev Yisrael"** ("Lover of Israel") be engraved on his tombstone — and his book of teachings is likewise called "Ohev Yisrael" (first printing 1863); an additional work, "Torat Emet," was published in 1854 [9], [10].
- After the death of R. Yaakov Yitzchak ("the Seer of Lublin," 1815), the Apter Rov was considered the elder of the tzaddikim of the generation in Poland-Galicia, and from him descended the Apt (Apta) dynasty, documented in the YIVO Encyclopedia [11].

**General background:** the tenure of the "Ohev Yisrael" made Opatów renowned in the Hasidic world — "Apta" among Hasidim to this day. In the century that followed, no dominant local court arose in the town, but rather — as documented in the yizkor book — a mosaic of shtiblekh of the courts of Ger, Aleksander, Modzhitz, Ostrowiec and Belz [7]. On the eve of the Holocaust the community was led by Rabbi Shalom Rokach, of the family of the Belz rebbes [7].
