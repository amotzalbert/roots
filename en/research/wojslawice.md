# The Jewish community of Wojsławice (וויסלביצה), Chełm district — 1760–1942

## 1. Chronological overview

### 1.1 The community's beginnings (15th–18th centuries)

Jews are first mentioned in Wojsławice in 1445 [3]. In 1531 the synagogue paid a tax of 15 florins, and in 1576 — 60 florins [3]; in that year (1531) 15 Jewish families liable for tax were recorded, and in 1564 about 125 souls were counted [4]. In 1588 "Meir, a Jewish barber from Wojsławice" was sued for non-payment of taxes [2]. In 1667, 16 Jewish families were counted out of about 60 households in the town [4]. In 1717 Jews constituted about 37% of the population [3]. On the eve of the blood libel, around 1761, the community numbered about 500 souls [4]. A first synagogue (most likely of wood) stood in the town from the beginning of the 17th century, around 1616 [1].

**[General background]** Wojsławice was a private town owned by nobility on the edge of the Chełm land, on the trade road between Chełm and Hrubieszów — a typical settlement pattern in which the patronage of the town's owners was what permitted (or denied) Jewish residence.

### 1.2 The blood libel of 1761 — the formative event

This is the harshest and most thoroughly documented event in the community's history, and it is considered one of the last and most notorious blood-libel trials in the Kingdom of Poland:

- **The background:** in 1760/1761 the body of a Christian child was found — Mikołaj, about three years old, son of Marcin and Katarzyna Andrzejuk from the nearby village of Czarnołozy [1][6][7].
- **The accusers:** the accusation was spread by members of the Frankist sect. A woman from among the Frankists, who impersonated the wife of one of the rabbis of Wojsławice, accused the community's leaders of ritual murder. According to the scholarly tradition, the Frankists — who settled in Wojsławice in 1761 at the instruction of Jacob Frank — sought to dispossess the town's Jews and take their place [3][6][7].
- **The accused:** the rabbis Sender Zyskieluk and Hershko Józefowicz (R. Hirsh son of Yosef), Leiba Moszkowicz known as "Sienicki," Josa Szymułowicz — together with the entire community [2][6][7].
- **The trial and the punishment:** the criminal court in Krasnystaw, after confessions extracted under torture, sentenced them to death by quartering while still alive; for the accused who accepted baptism into Christianity the sentence was commuted to beheading [1][6][7]. Five of the condemned were executed in the prison square in Krasnystaw [7]. Rabbi Józefowicz hanged himself in prison before the sentence was carried out; the court ruled that his body be dragged through the town's streets tied to a horse's tail and burned, and his ashes scattered [2][6].
- **The primary documentation:** the trial protocol was printed as early as 1761 under the Latin title *Processus judiciarius in causa patrati cruenti infanticidii per infideles judaeos seniores synagogae woyslavicensis* [6][8].
- **The outcome:** all the Jews of Wojsławice were expelled from the town [1][3][7]. The Frankists who settled in their place left as early as 1763, after a smallpox epidemic struck them [3].

### 1.3 The return and rebuilding (1780 onward)

In 1780 the town's new owner, Humbelina Kurdwanowska née Potocka, permitted the Jews to return and settle in Wojsławice [1][3][7]. In that period a brick-built synagogue was erected near the market square [3]. The recovery was slow: in 1777/1778 only 38–86 Jews were counted in the town [1][4].

### 1.4 The 19th century under Russian rule

**[General background]** From 1815 Wojsławice belonged to "Congress Poland" under the control of the Russian Empire, in the Lublin governorate (gubernia).

Rapid demographic growth [1][2][4]:

| Year | Total inhabitants | Jews | Percentage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1778 | 1,548 | 38 | ~2.5% |
| 1820 | 545 | 174 | ~32% |
| 1827 | 1,454 | 345 | 23.7% |
| 1857 | 1,557 | 841 | 54% |
| 1864 | — | — | ~75% |
| 1921 | 2,770 | 835 | 30% |

(The figures from 1778 and 1820 derive from different census methods; the gap between them also reflects the boundaries of the count — the town alone or together with the villages.)

In the second half of the 19th century the Jews were an absolute majority in the town. In 1890–1894 a new brick synagogue was built — a prayer hall for men in the east, and in the west an entrance room and a women's gallery; Hebrew inscriptions in it mark the year of its completion as 5663 (1902/3) [1][3][4]. The rabbis of the period: David London/Landman, Baruch Yisrael, Meir Shraga Feivel Weinstein (from 1906), and the last of the town's rabbis — Yaakov Yitzchak Citrinboim, who perished in the Holocaust [2][4].

**Hasidism:** shtiblekh of the Trisk (Turzysk), Belz and Ger Hasidim operated in the town [2][4][5]. The Pinkas Hakehillot also mentions R. Avner, a Hasid of the "Seer of Lublin," among the town's early rabbis [2].

### 1.5 World War I

In 1914 Russian Cossacks carried out pogroms against the town's Jews; the Austrian occupation (1915) restored order but brought severe economic hardship [2].

### 1.6 Between the two world wars (1918–1939)

With the establishment of independent Poland, harassment by Polish soldiers was recorded — cutting of beards and confiscation of property; during the brief Soviet occupation of 1920, Jews were accused of collaboration [2]. In 1921, 835 Jews were counted (30% of the population), and in 1922 the registered community numbered 1,396 souls (including the surrounding villages) [1][4]. In 1928 Jews constituted 57% of the residents of the town itself [3].

Vibrant public life: branches of Zionist movements and Left Poalei Zion, a Betar chapter (from 1928), a branch of Agudat Yisrael and a "Beit Yaakov" school for girls, a "Tarbut" association, two libraries, four cheders (1919), drama circles and mutual-aid organizations; from the beginning of the 20th century a Jewish savings-and-credit fund operated [2][4][5]. Office-holders mentioned: the rabbis David London, Meir-Feivel Weinstein and Yaakov Citrinboim, and the cantor Falik Zucker [4].

The memoirs of Mendel Schaffer/Schafir, "Sixty Years of My Life in Wojsławice 1879–1939," printed in the community's memorial (yizkor) book (Tel Aviv 1970), are the most detailed insider account of town life in this period — winter in the town, village life, self-defense and the encounter with Zionism [5].

### 1.7 The Holocaust: occupation, ghetto, Sobibór (1939–1943)

- **September 11, 1939:** the German army entered the town [2].
- About 150 Jews were sent to forced labor in the Żmudź marshes, and about 80 — to the construction of the Bełżec camp [2]. The synagogue was converted into stables [2]; the beit midrash was converted in 1940 into a mill [4]. A Judenrat was established headed by Einhorn, and an open (unfenced) ghetto was created [2].
- **May 25, 1940:** SS men executed 12 residents and 100 were deported to Germany (of all the town's residents) [3].
- **February 12, 1942:** a fire consumed most of the Jews' houses and the kosher slaughterhouse [1].
- **Early January 1942:** 50–60 elderly Jews were deported via Włodawa to the Sobibór extermination camp [2].
- **October 1942 — the community's liquidation:** all the Jews of Wojsławice were marched on foot out of the town and deported to Sobibór. According to the documentation of "Teatr NN" they were taken to Chełm and from there sent to Sobibór [1]; according to the Pinkas Hakehillot they were deported via Włodawa to Sobibór, among them the last rabbi, Yaakov Yitzchak Citrinboim [2]. In that month the largest execution at the site took place — 60 captured Jews were shot in the meadows north of the market square; murders of those in hiding continued into 1943 as well [1][2]. The community was annihilated completely [3].

**[General background]** Only a handful of survivors remained — through flight eastward, in hiding or in the camps; the yizkor book contains testimonies of "Wojsławice–Łódź–Auschwitz" routes and of paths of rescue reaching Haifa [5].

After the war: the old synagogue, converted into a mill, was demolished in 1981; the new synagogue (1890/1903) was converted in 1950 into a warehouse [3], and was later restored and stands to this day.

## 2. Daily life and livelihoods

- **Market and fairs:** the weekly market days were held on Tuesdays; from 1755 four annual fairs were permitted [4]. **[General background]** the economy of such a town rested on mediation between the Ukrainian and Polish peasants of the surroundings and urban goods — the classic "shtetl" pattern.
- **Documented occupations:** bakers (the baker Cywich), shoemaker-cobblers and hide merchants (Feivel Schild "Pavke"), an owner of a liquor warehouse (Bergier), shopkeepers, tailors, barber-surgeons, textile printers, and owners of oil and grain mills [1][4]. In the 19th century workshops and wool factories operated in the town [2].
- **Institutions:** two synagogues adjacent to one another (the old one from about 1780 and the new one from 1890–1903), a beit midrash, a kosher slaughterhouse, four cheders, two libraries, a savings-and-credit fund, and two cemeteries — the old one from the mid-16th century and the new one from the mid-19th century on Grabowiecka Street [1][3][4].
- **A mixed population:** on the eve of World War II about 3,200 residents lived in the town — Poles, Jews and Ukrainians [3].
