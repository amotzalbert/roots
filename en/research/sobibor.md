# The Destruction of the Jewish Communities of the Lublin District (Wojsławice, Chełm) and the Sobibór Extermination Camp — 1939–1943

## 1. The German Occupation and the Establishment of the Ghettos

### 1.1 The Occupation (September 1939)

The German army invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. Wojsławice was captured on **11 September 1939** [1]. Chełm, as a district center, was captured in those same days of September 1939 and subsequently came under German occupation rule — it was included in the "Lublin District" (Distrikt Lublin) within the Generalgouvernement (General Government), the region of occupied Poland that was not directly annexed to the Reich [2][3].

**[General background]** Under the rule of Odilo Globocnik, the SS and Police Leader of the district, the Lublin District became a central hub in the planning and execution of the murder of the Jews of the Generalgouvernement — both as a concentration zone for deported Jews from 1939–1941, and, from 1942 onward, as the focal point of the extermination itself within the framework of "Operation Reinhard" (see Chapter 2).

### 1.2 The Wojsławice Ghetto

In Wojsławice an open (unfenced) ghetto was established at the beginning of the occupation, under the authority of a Judenrat headed by Einhorn; the assessment in the research literature is that the "establishment of the ghetto" took place in **1940** [1][4]. About 150 local Jews were sent to forced labor in the Żmudź marshes, and about 80 more — to the construction of the Bełżec camp. The synagogue was converted into stables and the beit midrash was converted in 1940 into a mill [1]. On **25 May 1940** SS men executed 12 residents, and 100 were deported to Germany [3]. Ghetto population: 950 persons in 1939, 1,015 in June 1941, 1,019 in July 1942 [4]. On **12 February 1942** a fire consumed most of the Jewish houses and the kosher slaughterhouse [1]. Further details on ghetto conditions and on the history of the community up to 1939 are compiled in the report `wojslawice.md`.

### 1.3 The Chełm Ghetto

The ghetto in Chełm was established on **30 October 1940** as an "open ghetto" in a poor neighborhood at the edge of the city; toward the end of 1941 it became a closed ghetto [5]. Ghetto population: about 15,000 Jews on the eve of September 1939; about 11,000 in April 1941 (after some were deported or fled); about 12,500 in December 1941 (after the concentration of Jews from nearby localities) [5]. From the beginning of 1941 until the end of March 1941, dozens of Jews were deported from Chełm to the Włodawa ghetto [3]. The ghetto's inhabitants suffered from hunger, disease, and arbitrary murder at the hands of the occupiers [6]. On **12 January 1940** — at an even earlier stage, before the formal establishment of the ghetto — more than 400 victims were executed in Chełm (most likely as part of the murder of prisoners of war and the deportation/murder of the sick, not exclusively of Jews; this detail should be verified against an additional source) [5].

## 2. "Operation Reinhard" and the Sobibór Extermination Camp

### 2.1 Operation Reinhard

**[General background]** "Operation Reinhard" (Aktion Reinhard/Reinhardt) is the name given in retrospect to the systematic German plan for the extermination of the Jews of the Generalgouvernement (including the Lublin District), carried out in three extermination camps established specifically for this purpose: Bełżec, Sobibór, and Treblinka. The operation is named after Reinhard Heydrich, who was assassinated in May 1942 [7][8].

### 2.2 The Sobibór Camp — Establishment and Structure

The Sobibór camp was built by SS and German police forces **in the spring of 1942**, in the eastern part of the Lublin District, near the border with the then Ukraine/Belarus, on the Chełm–Włodawa railway line [8]. Regular gassing operations began **in early May 1942**, after initial trials [8]. The camp was divided into three zones: Camp I (administration and workshops), Camp II (reception and undressing), and Camp III (the extermination area — the gas chambers and burial pits); the zones were connected by a narrow, enclosed passage called "the tube" (Schlauch) [8]. Camp commandants: Franz Stangl (until August 1942), followed by Franz Reichleitner [8].

### 2.3 The Method of Extermination

The victims were killed with carbon monoxide gas (a byproduct of internal combustion engines) piped into chambers disguised as showers; those unable to walk were shot at open pits [8].

### 2.4 Total Number of Victims

According to the USHMM estimate: **at least 167,000 people** were murdered at Sobibór from April 1942 until mid-October 1943 [8]. Other estimates in the literature (for example Wikipedia, citing various sources) range between **170,000 and 250,000** [9]. It should be noted that this is a range of estimates and not a closed, agreed-upon figure; the gap stems, among other things, from the loss of a significant portion of the German documentation. Among the camp's victims — Jews from the northern and eastern Lublin District (including Chełm and Wojsławice), from the occupied territories of the Soviet Union, from Germany, Austria, Slovakia, Bohemia-Moravia, the Netherlands, and France [8].

### 2.5 The Sobibór Uprising and the Closure of the Camp — October 1943

On **14 October 1943**, around 4:00 p.m., the camp's prisoners launched an organized revolt: in small groups they lured SS officers into storerooms and workshops under various pretexts and killed them with axes and sharp tools — among them the deputy camp commandant Johann Niemann, killed in the tailors' workshop, and SS man Josef Wulf, killed in the clothing warehouse. In total, 11 SS men were killed [10]. About 300 prisoners broke through the barbed-wire fence and tried to escape through the minefield surrounding the camp; only about 50 of them survived until the end of the war [10]. Following the uprising, the camp was dismantled by prisoners brought there for that purpose from Treblinka, and they themselves were murdered in November 1943 to eliminate witnesses; the camp grounds were plowed over and planted with a pine forest [8][10]. "Operation Reinhard" was declared officially concluded in November 1943 [7].

## 3. The Specific Deportation Dates from Wojsławice and Chełm to Sobibór

### 3.1 Wojsławice

- **Early January 1942:** 50–60 elderly Jews were deported via Włodawa to the Sobibór extermination camp [1][4].
- **20 April 1942:** deportation of 209 people to labor camps (not directly to Sobibór) [4].
- **October 1942 — liquidation of the ghetto:** all the Jews of Wojsławice were marched on foot out of the town and deported to Sobibór. Different sources disagree on the details of the route and the exact number: according to "Teatr NN" they were first taken to Chełm and from there sent to Sobibór, and the number is estimated at about 1,200 deportees [4]; according to Pinkas Hakehillot Polin they were deported via Włodawa to Sobibór, among them the town's last rabbi, Yaakov Yitzhak Cytrynbaum [1]. In that same month, 60 Jews caught in hiding were shot to death in the meadows north of the market square; the murder of those in hiding continued into 1943 as well [1][4]. It should be noted that the figure "about 12,000" appearing in one of the search summaries (a general search engine) is implausible relative to the size of the entire community (about 1,000 persons in 1942) and is not supported by the primary source (Teatr NN); it appears to be an automatic-summarization error and is not included in this report.

### 3.2 Chełm

According to Teatr NN and according to Yad Vashem, the series of deportations from Chełm to Sobibór included:

- **21–23 May 1942** (eve of Shavuot): about 4,300 Jews were deported to Sobibór, among them about 1,000 elderly people from Chełm itself; among the deportees were also about 2,000 Jews who had been brought shortly beforehand from Slovakia [6][12]. In that same Aktion, about 200 people were shot on the spot [12].
- **End of June 1942:** a further deportation of 300–600 people, after Gestapo men from Sobibór demanded workers for the camp [6][12].
- **Mid-August 1942:** 3,000–4,000 Jews were deported to Sobibór [12].
- **5 October 1942:** an additional 1,000 Jews from the Chełm ghetto were deported to Sobibór [6].
- **27–28 October 1942:** deportation of about 3,300 Jews (local residents and Jewish refugees) — according to one source the destination was Włodawa (not necessarily Sobibór directly; it may have been a transit station) [6].
- **6–9 November 1942 — liquidation of the ghetto:** more than 2,000–3,000 Jews were deported to Sobibór in the final deportation wave that closed the open ghetto; about 1,000 Jewish craftsmen remained in a labor camp [11][12].
- **January and March 1943:** additional, smaller deportations of the remnants of the labor camp to Sobibór [11][12].
- **March 1943:** the remaining Jewish population in Chełm fell to 28 people, and in April 1943 to just 8; this remnant was murdered at the Jewish cemetery on Kolejowa Street [11][12].

**Reliability note:** among the sources (Teatr NN, Yad Vashem, and the municipal gov.pl portal) there are slight differences in the numbers and in the exact dates of some of the deportations (for example, the range "6–9 November" versus "6 November" alone). The report presents the ranges as they appeared in the sources, and does not reconcile them arbitrarily.

## 4. What Happened to the Relatives Found in the Tree

The family research already conducted (the Moskal and Schafir/Szafir branches from Wojsławice) shows that **none of the direct family members documented so far perished in the Holocaust** — the family fled eastward into Soviet territory before, or close to, the German occupation of the region. This chapter therefore does not deal with the fate of the direct family, but with the background from which it fled: the entire community — including those of the Moskal and Schafir households who remained in place — was destroyed in the wave of deportations described in Chapters 1–3 above.

According to the family tree (a family source, not a primary archival source located in this report): **Judka-Lejba Moskal and Cywia-Dwora Alspektor perished in 1942**. A search conducted as part of this research in the public source databases detailed above (Yad Vashem, USHMM, sztetl.org.pl) did not verify an individual record for these two family members — their names were not found registered in Yad Vashem's "Pages of Testimony" database or in the parallel databases via a general web search. **This should not be taken as a contradiction of the family tree's testimony**: the Yad Vashem database ("The Central Database of Shoah Victims' Names") is fully searchable by name only through the site itself (yvng.yadvashem.org), and was not fully scanned in the general web search performed here — a direct, focused search on the Yad Vashem site is required (including alternative spellings of the name, such as Judka-Lejba/Juda-Lejb Moskal and Cywia-Dwora Alspektor/Alsfektor) in order to confirm or refute an individual record.

In the absence of a verified individual record, only the general factual framework can be established: if Judka-Lejba Moskal and Cywia-Dwora Alspektor were in Wojsławice at the time of the deportation (October 1942), their probable fate — in accordance with the documentary pattern of the deportation as described in Section 3.1 — is deportation to Sobibór and immediate extermination upon arrival, in accordance with the camp's method of operation described in Section 2.3. This is a logical reconstruction based on the documented pattern of events, **not direct archival confirmation** of their individual fate, and it must be marked as such in any future use of this material.

**Recommendation for follow-up research:** a direct search at yvng.yadvashem.org (the Pages of Testimony database) and in the Arolsen Archives database for the names "Moskal" and "Alsfektor/Alspektor" in the Wojsławice/Chełm context, in order to try to locate a Page of Testimony or a primary documentary record supporting the date and circumstances of death that appear in the family tree.
