# Rawa Ruska (ראווה רוסקה): the Jewish community, 1780–1870

**Background report for the family-history site — the Szaufer branch**
A district town in Eastern Galicia, the Austrian Empire (today Rava-Ruska, Lviv oblast, Ukraine). About 50 km north-west of Lviv (Lwów), very close to the border line that separated, after 1815, Austrian Galicia from Russian Congress Poland.

## 2. The life of Galician Jewry: taxes, restrictions and poverty

### 2.1 The turning point of 1772 [background + sources]

With the annexation of Galicia to Austria in 1772, Vienna inherited a poor province containing the largest concentration of Jews in the empire. The Austrian regime viewed the Jews as a "problem" to be "corrected": on the one hand enlightened reforms (German-language schools, military service, the obligation of German family names in 1787 — hence most likely also the form Szaufer/שאופר), and on the other a crushing tax press intended both to fill the treasury and to reduce the number of "poor" Jews. The Rawa Ruska yizkor book sums up: the fate of Rawa's Jews was the fate of all the communities of Galicia — the flood of Austrian decrees sowed mainly chaos [source: yizkor book, JewishGen].

### 2.2 The system of special taxes [sources]

On the Jews of Galicia were imposed, in addition to the general taxes, taxes unique to Jews alone [sources: JewishEncyclopedia entry Galicia; the Zolynia site on Galicia]:

- **Toleration tax (Toleranzsteuer)** — a payment for the very right to reside in the land; abolished in 1797.
- **The kosher-meat tax (Fleischsteuer / krupka)** — a levy on every kilogram of meat slaughtered kosher. It made meat so expensive that in poor households it became a Sabbath-only food.
- **The candle tax (Lichtzündungssteuer)**, introduced in 1797 in place of the toleration tax: 2 kreuzer for every Sabbath and festival candle, 6 kreuzer for a yahrzeit candle, 10 kreuzer for Yom Kippur candles, and half a kreuzer for a Hanukkah candle [source: Zolynia/Galicia]. A tax on a religious commandment — that is, a tax that could not be evaded without giving up a foundation of Jewish life. Its collection was farmed out to Jewish lessees ("factors"), a system that sowed bitterness within the communities themselves.
- **Marriage tax and marriage license** — civil marriage was permitted only with a paid license, and from a certain stage also subject to an examination in knowledge of German and of Herz Homberg's book "Bnei Zion" [background; see also WMJGS]. The documented result: most Galician Jews married in religious-only marriages ("rabbi marriages") without civil registration [source: WMJGS], and therefore many children were registered under the mother's family name — a genealogical trap familiar to every researcher of Galicia.
- Ancillary taxes: a synagogue-building tax, a cemetery tax and more [source: Zolynia/Galicia].

The special taxes were finally abolished only in the revolution of 1848, and full civil equality arrived with the constitution of December 1867 [source: JewishVirtualLibrary].

### 2.3 Livelihood restrictions and poverty [sources + background]

At the end of the 18th century the authorities forbade Jews to reside in villages and to keep inns and liquor distilleries — a central branch of livelihood. The communities of Galicia, Rawa Ruska among them, petitioned for the decree's repeal and were rejected [source: yizkor book, JewishGen]. Joseph II attempted to direct Jews to agriculture and established Jewish agricultural colonies in the Rawa area as well, but few joined [source: yizkor book, JewishGen].

In Rawa Ruska itself the Jews made their living mainly from retail trade, crafts and tavern-keeping: tailors, furriers and hatters, workers in mills and beer breweries, and a pottery factory whose products earned a reputation [source: yizkor book, JewishGen].

**[Background]** The general picture in 19th-century Galicia is one of mass poverty: growing demographic density, petty livelihoods of brokerage and peddling, and ever-harsher competition. The expression "Galician misery" (Galizianer Elend) became proverbial. This is the economic background that every discussion of emigration from Galicia — westward to America at the century's end, or northward to Congress Poland at its beginning — must take into account.

### 2.4 Belz Hasidism and the religious atmosphere [sources]

Around 1817, R. Shalom Rokeach (1783–1855), a disciple of the "Seer of Lublin," established his court in the town of Bełz — at a distance of only about 25 km from Rawa Ruska. Belz became the center of Hasidism in Galicia, and thousands of Hasidim, among them rabbis and rebbes, streamed to it from Galicia, Poland and Hungary. R. Shalom also served as the spokesman of Galician Jewry in the struggle over its economic condition, and fought the Haskalah [source: YIVO Encyclopedia, entry Belz Hasidic Dynasty]. The yizkor book explicitly notes the deep influence of the Belz court on Rawa Ruska, where the Great Synagogue, kloyzn and many batei midrash operated, and where the few maskilim met fierce opposition [source: yizkor book, JewishGen].

**[Background]** The meaning for our purposes: a Jewish family living in Rawa Ruska in the generation of Szmul and Estera Szaufer (late 18th – mid 19th century) almost certainly lived in a Hasidic or Hasidism-influenced environment, within a single day's travel of the Belz court at the height of its flourishing.

## 3. The migration from Galicia to Congress Poland — the context of Chaim Szaufer

This is the heart of the report as far as the family story is concerned: Chaim Szaufer, born in Rawa Ruska around 1804, appears in Wojsławice in the Lublin governorate (Congress Poland, under Russia) by the 1830s at the latest — that is, he crossed the Austrian–Russian border northward, a distance of about 75 km as the crow flies between Rawa Ruska and Wojsławice.

### 3.1 Why did young Jews leave Galicia in the early 19th century? [background, anchored in the sources of section 2]

The movement from Galicia to Congress Poland in the first decades of the 19th century is a phenomenon well known in the literature and in genealogical documentation, and its causes derive directly from the documented facts above:

1. **The marriage taxes and marriage restrictions.** In Galicia it cost money — and at times also a German examination — to marry lawfully. In Congress Poland there were no parallel restrictions on marriage itself. For a young man in his twenties (Chaim was ~25 in 1829) this is a very practical consideration.
2. **Austrian compulsory conscription.** The Jews of Austria were obligated to military service from 1788 [source: yizkor book, JewishGen]. In Congress Poland, by contrast, Jews were exempt from military service in exchange for a "recruitment tax" until 1843. The flight of young men from conscription is one of the most documented motives for internal Jewish migrations in this period.
3. **The candle tax and the meat tax** — a daily burden that had no identical counterpart on the Russian side.
4. **Economic opportunity.** The Chełm–Zamość region across the border was an agricultural expanse opening up to Jewish commercial activity, and private towns like Wojsławice attracted artisans and merchants. The border itself created a border economy — trade (and also smuggling) between the two empires.
5. **Geographic and cultural proximity.** This was not "long-distance emigration": the same Yiddish, the same extended families on both sides of a line drawn only in 1815, cutting through a single living space that until 1772 had been one kingdom.

### 3.2 The status of the "foreigners": Austrian subjects in Russian Poland [background]

Migrants like Chaim Szaufer did not automatically become Russian subjects. In civil-status records in Congress Poland, natives of Galicia frequently appear with their origin noted ("from Austrian Galicia," "Austrian subject" — zagraniczny, z Galicji austriackiej). This is precisely the phenomenon reflected in Chaim's death act of 1866: even after decades in Wojsławice, the registrar saw fit to note that he was born "in the town of Rawa in Austrian Galicia." Such a notation is a genealogical finding of great value — it almost always reflects a declaration by the family itself or documents they carried with them, and it strengthens the reliability of the identification of the town of origin.

**[Note of caution]** No migration documents or travel papers of Chaim himself have been located so far; the exact date and his specific reason for migrating remain an informed conjecture based on the historical context above.

### 3.3 World War I — in brief [sources]

Rawa Ruska, a border town and railway junction, stood in 1914–1915 at the heart of the fighting between Austria and Russia (the "Battle of Rawa Ruska," September 1914). The community was severely harmed by bombardments, conscriptions and cholera and typhus epidemics; after the war, during the Polish–Ukrainian war and the Polish occupation, the Jews suffered abuse, forced labor and the cutting of beards at the hands of soldiers of Haller's army. The visit of Sir Stuart Samuel in 1919 at the head of a British commission of inquiry reflected the international attention to the pogroms [source: yizkor book, JewishGen].

### 3.4 The Holocaust — closure [sources]

Rawa Ruska's fate was sealed by its proximity to the Bełżec extermination camp, about 20 km away. In March 1942, with the start of the murders at Bełżec, the first transport left Rawa Ruska — about a thousand Jews; at the end of July 1942 about 2,000 more were deported. In August 1942 the Jews were concentrated in an open ghetto in three streets in the poor outskirts of the town. Between December 7 and 11, 1942, about 5,000 Jews were murdered by shooting in the liquidation Aktion, and in June 1943 the last Jews of the ghetto and the labor camp were shot in the Borove forest west of the town [source: Holocaust Historical Society]. A community centuries old — the one in which Szmul, Estera and Chaim Szaufer were born and lived — ceased to exist. The murder sites have been marked in recent years within the framework of the "Protecting Memory" project.
