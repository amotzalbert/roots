## 0. Cultural background: the "Land of Chelm" and the "Wise Men of Chelm"

Chełm is known in Ashkenazi Jewish folklore as "the town of the foolish sages" (di chelmer chachomim) — a cycle of folk jokes in which the town's residents convene for long and earnest deliberation (at times consulting holy books) in order to arrive at absurd conclusions, such as a plan to "steal the moon" to secure a steady income for the town. This genre received literary treatments by Sholem Aleichem, I. L. Peretz, Menachem Kipnis (the "Khelemer Mayses" anthology — חלמער מעשׂיות, 1930) and, above all, Isaac Bashevis Singer ("The Wise Men of Chelm and the Foolish Fish" and others). The origin of the joke is not known with certainty — according to a prevailing hypothesis in folklore scholarship, it is an adaptation of the "town of fools" joke tradition common in many cultures (such as the English "Men of Gotham") that became fixed on Chełm for reasons that are unclear, perhaps precisely because the town was **a genuine center of Torah learning** — the gap between the "ridiculous" reputation and its true standing as one of the significant Jewish communities of Poland is part of the irony that perpetuated the joke. It must be stressed: this is **cultural-folkloric context only**, and does not bear on the historical documentation of the community presented below.
Source: [Teatr NN – Leksykon: Chełm](https://teatrnn.pl/leksykon/artykuly/chelm/); [Wirtualny Chełm – "O mędrcach z Chełma"](https://wirtualnychelm.pl/o-medrcach-z-chelma-co-ukradli-ksiezyc-zeby-wypromowac-miasto/).

## 1. Chronology

### Beginnings of the settlement and the community's size (up to 1780)
Jewish presence in Chełm is documented as early as the 16th century. According to Teatr NN, in the 16th century a "chief rabbi of the Jewish communities of the region" (Aharon Yehuda) was active in the town, and to the figure of Aharon Eliyahu ben Yehuda, known as "Eliyahu Ba'al Shem" (c. 1550), Jewish legend attributes **the earliest tradition of the creation of a golem** — earlier even than the Prague golem tradition. This detail should be treated as **a tradition/legend documented in the scholarly literature on folklore**, not as proven historical fact. In the 16th century, R. Efraim ben Yosef (born 1572) also composed Torah works in Chełm.
Source: [Teatr NN – Leksykon: Chełm](https://teatrnn.pl/leksykon/artykuly/chelm/).

**No documented numerical figure was located for the size of Chełm's Jewish community in the year 1780 itself** — systematic statistical documentation (revisions/official counts) begins from the early 19th century.

### The 19th century — rapid growth
| Year | Jews | % of total population | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1827 | 1,902 | 68% | Wirtualny Sztetl |
| 1857 | 2,493 | 68% | Wirtualny Sztetl |
| 1897 | 7,226 | 56% | Wirtualny Sztetl |

Between 1827 and 1897 the community grew roughly 3.8-fold — a reflection of intensified Jewish migration into the town, which over the course of the 19th century became a leading commercial-religious center in the eastern Lublin district.
Source: [Wirtualny Sztetl – Chełm, history of the community](https://sztetl.org.pl/pl/miejscowosci/c/41-chelm/99-historia-spolecznosci/137157-historia-spolecznosci).

### Early 20th century and the interwar period
| Year | Jews | % of total population | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1921 | 12,074 | 52% | Wirtualny Sztetl |
| 1931 | 13,537 | 46.5% | Wirtualny Sztetl |
| 1939 | ~14,995–15,000 | 44.6% (out of 33,622 residents) | Wirtualny Sztetl |

In the interwar period, according to Wirtualny Sztetl, Chełm was **the second-largest Jewish concentration in the Lublin district after the city of Lublin itself**.
Source: [Wirtualny Sztetl – Chełm, history of the community](https://sztetl.org.pl/pl/miejscowosci/c/41-chelm/99-historia-spolecznosci/137157-historia-spolecznosci).

### World War II — ghetto and destruction (1939–1943)
- **Autumn 1939**: German occupation; Chełm passes between German and Soviet hands in the early phase of the occupation (Lublin district).
- **October 1940**: establishment of the Chełm ghetto, in which about 11,000 Jews were concentrated (a decline from the total pre-war figure, apparently due to flight eastward in 1939–1941 and early mortality/expulsions).
- **21–22 May 1942**: start of the first deportation Aktion from the ghetto — about 4,000 Jews were deported to the Sobibór extermination camp.
- **June 1942**: a second Aktion, carried out by Gestapo men from Sobibór; after a selection, about 600 Jews "fit for work" were deported to Sobibór.
- **5 October 1942**: a further deportation of about 1,000 Jews to Sobibór.
- **27–28 October 1942**: about 3,300 Jews were marched toward Włodawa; many were murdered during the march, and the survivors were deported to Sobibór.
- **6–9 November 1942**: the final liquidation of the ghetto — about 3,000 were murdered on the spot, and about 4,000 were deported to Sobibór.
- About 1,000 Jewish craftsmen remained in a forced-labor camp; most were deported to Sobibór in early 1943, and the others were murdered in March 1943 at the Jewish cemetery of Chełm.
- According to estimates, about 500 Jews of Chełm survived the Holocaust.

It should be noted that the exact figures for each deportation wave vary slightly between the sources (Wirtualny Sztetl, Teatr NN, Yad Vashem) — the table above reflects the generally accepted overall picture; for precise verification by date, the primary source should be consulted.
Sources: [Wirtualny Sztetl – Getto w Chełmie](https://sztetl.org.pl/pl/miejscowosci/c/41-chelm/116-miejsca-martyrologii/45205-getto-w-chelmie); [Teatr NN – Chełm, akcja "Reinhardt"](https://teatrnn.pl/ar/chelm-akcja-reinhardt/); [Yad Vashem – The History of the Chełm Jewish Community](https://www.yadvashem.org/communities/chelm.html); [Yad Vashem – Roundups in the Chełm Ghetto](https://www.yadvashem.org/communities/chelm/destruction-of-the-community.html); [portal gov.pl – 80. rocznica likwidacji getta w Chełmie](https://samorzad.gov.pl/web/miasto-chelm/80-rocznica-likwidacji-getta-w-chelmie).

## 2. Daily life, institutions, and livelihoods

### Religious and communal institutions
- **The Great Synagogue** — established in the 16th century.
- **The beit midrash (study house)** — originating in the 16th century; rebuilt in 1914.
- **The Small Synagogue** (8 Kopernika St.) — built 1912–1914 from donations of the local Jewish public, in Art Nouveau style; partially burned by the German army in World War II, and partially restored in 1955 (used as a flour warehouse).
- A mikveh (ritual bath) and a Jewish cemetery.
- Before World War I, **six private batei midrash** operated in the town; in the interwar period their number grew to **about 21 private prayer houses**, located mainly on Adrianowska, Lubelska, Szkolna, and Wesoła streets.
Sources: [Wirtualny Sztetl – Chełm](https://sztetl.org.pl/pl/miejscowosci/c/41-chelm/99-historia-spolecznosci/137157-historia-spolecznosci); [Virtual Shtetl – Synagoga Mała w Chełmie](https://sztetl.org.pl/en/towns/c/41-chelm/112-synagogues-houses-of-prayer-and-others/83387-synagoga-mala-w-chelmie-ul-kopernika-8); [Teatr NN – Leksykon: Chełm](https://teatrnn.pl/leksykon/artykuly/chelm/).

### Organizations and public life between the wars
Numerous social organizations operated in the town, including the TOZ society (Society for the Protection of the Health of the Jews), the Women's International Zionist Organization (WIZO), trade unions, and sports organizations. In the 1927 city-council elections the Jewish lists won 10 seats, among them the Jewish National Bloc, Poalei Zion Right, Poalei Zion Left, the organization of independent craftsmen, and the Bund. Local Jewish press and educational institutions operated in the town, including a public school and a humanistic gymnasium.
Source: [Wirtualny Sztetl – Chełm](https://sztetl.org.pl/pl/miejscowosci/c/41-chelm/99-historia-spolecznosci/137157-historia-spolecznosci); [Teatr NN – Leksykon: Chełm](https://teatrnn.pl/leksykon/artykuly/chelm/).

### Livelihoods and economy
Most of the Jewish residents made their living from petty trade and handicrafts, alongside a smaller stratum of the affluent and the educated. Data from 1927 show that **all the businesses for buying and selling cattle** were Jewish-owned, as well as most of the grain and flour warehouses, ten bakeries, eight butcher shops, six food wholesalers, and two iron-goods wholesalers. In industry: the owner of the town's largest industrial plant — the "Chełm Distillery and Vodka Factory" — was Adolf Dauman; a factory for agricultural machinery was owned by Hersz Blumenfeld; the Rozen brothers operated a carbonated-beverages plant. Jews were owners of, or partners in, flour mills, dairies/creameries, vinegar distilleries, oil presses, soap factories, and a small candy factory.
Source: [Wirtualny Sztetl – Chełm](https://sztetl.org.pl/pl/miejscowosci/c/41-chelm/99-historia-spolecznosci/137157-historia-spolecznosci) (the 1927 and 1939 data are quoted via a search of the portal).

## 3. The connection to Wojsławice

**Documented facts (general geographic-historical background):**
- Wojsławice lies about **26 km** south of Chełm, in the Chełm powiat (powiat chełmski), Lublin district.
- Wojsławice was a town holding urban rights from 1437/1439–1443 until 1869 (when it was demoted to the status of a village/small town) — an important regional trade-route junction before that.
- Jewish presence in Wojsławice is documented in the literature from approximately 1445; in the interwar period the Jews were **the numerically dominant ethnic group** in the town.
Sources: [Wikipedia (EN) – Gmina Wojsławice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gmina_Wojs%C5%82awice); [Wikipedia (EN) – Wojsławice, Lublin Voivodeship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wojs%C5%82awice,_Lublin_Voivodeship); [Teatr NN – Wojsławice, historia miejscowości](https://teatrnn.pl/leksykon/artykuly/wojslawice-historia-miejscowosci/).

**Assessment/context (not a directly documented fact):**
A distance of about 26 km between the smaller town (Wojsławice) and the district center (Chełm) is a distance typical of the local community network in the Lublin–Chełm region, in which trading families, matchmaking, and livelihood migration created close inter-communal ties between a small town and the district center. This fits a general pattern across most of the Lublin region, but **we currently possess no specific record** (a marriage record, a joint commercial registration, etc.) documenting a direct link between the Bukszpan/Alspektor branches and Wojsławice itself. To be clear: the family's documented center (177 JRI-Poland records) is **Chełm**, not Wojsławice. A direct family/commercial link to Wojsławice **has not yet been verified** and requires further checking in the Geneteka/JRI-Poland records (marriages, wedding witnesses, business partners).
