# The Jews of the Villages on the Banks of the Bug in the Chełm District: Świerże, Dorohusk and Żmudź — 1780–1880

> Research report for the family-history site — every documented fact is accompanied by a source number [n] from the list in Chapter 5. Paragraphs marked **[General background]** are accepted historical context from the research literature, without a point-specific source citation — and should not be relied upon as primary documentation. Where the sources are silent, it is stated explicitly: **not known**. Very little documentation has survived for small villages like these, and what was not found — was not invented.

---

## 1. The Region and the Villages

The three settlements sit in a narrow triangle at the eastern edge of the Chełm district (powiat chełmski), on the western bank of the Bug River and in its hinterland: Świerże and Dorohusk on the river itself, about 8 km apart; Żmudź about 15 km southwest of them, inland. Chełm — the district town and the large Jewish community of the region — lies about 25 km to the west. Wojsławice, the town to which the family migrated in the 19th century, lies about 30 km southwest of Świerże.

### 1.1 Świerże

- First mention: 1443, in connection with a church endowment by the boyar Piotr Wołczko Rokut; town rights were granted around that time — according to sztetl.org.pl around 1435 [1], according to the Polish Wikipedia before 1455 [2]. In other words: for some four hundred years Świerże was a **small town** (miasteczko), not a village.
- A private town under changing noble ownership: Jełowicki (1625), Olędzki (1694–1782), Przyłucki (1782), the Orsetti family from 1780; after 1760 the owner Rulikowski built a palace there [1].
- **Thanks to its location on the Bug, a large river harbor (przystań rzeczna) developed in Świerże** [1].
- The partitions of Poland: 1795 under Austrian control, 1807 in the Duchy of Warsaw, 1815 in Congress Poland [1].
- On 19 November 1822 Świerże lost its town rights and officially became an "osada" (a semi-urban settlement) [1][2]. In the years 1867–1954 it was the seat of the gmina authority (gmina Świerże) [2].
- Economy in the 19th century: small-scale industry — water mills, a distillery (gorzelnia), a brickworks, windmills, a tar distillery [1]. In 1852 a cholera epidemic struck the place [1].
- 9 July 1863: the Battle of Świerże in the Polish uprising (the January Uprising) — a Polish force under Major Władysław Ruciński forced the Russians to retreat [1][2].

### 1.2 Dorohusk

- First mention: 1246/1250, in the Ruthenian chronicle — Prince Daniel Romanovich received envoys at the local stronghold [3][4].
- A village and estate (wś i dwór) on the left bank of the Bug, with a Catholic parish church (parafia Dorohusk) and a shelter for the elderly and disabled; in the Chełm district, gmina Turka [5].
- **1827 census: 90 houses, 644 residents** (Słownik geograficzny, vol. II, p. 124) [5]. The religious breakdown of this figure — **not known**; no source stating how many of them were Jews has been located.
- In 1867 the gmina of Dorohusk was established; in 1874 its name was changed to gmina Turka [4].
- The Dorohusk page on the Wirtualny Sztetl portal exists at a fixed address but at the time of checking (August 2026) does not load and displays no content — so there is currently no Jewish community entry available for Dorohusk on the portal [6].

### 1.3 Żmudź

- First mention: 1443 (affiliation to a Catholic parish); in 1505 the name was written Zmodz; in 1570 it was a village of the Chełm starostwo (royal estate) [7].
- The village's name is identical to the name of the Lithuanian region of Żmudź (Žemaitija/Samogitia) — most likely in connection with the Samogitian origin of the boyars who received lands in the area in the 15th century (Wołczko Rokut, founder of the Świerże church, is called in the sources a "Samogitian boyar") [1][7].
- 1827 census data for Żmudź itself — **not located in an accessible source**. For comparison, the nearby village of Dryszczów in gmina Żmudź numbered in 1827: 20 houses and 140 residents [5].
- Direct documentation of Jews in Żmudź in the 19th century — **not located**, apart from the family evidence: Moszek Szyfman is recorded in 1832 as residing in the "village of Żmudź/Dorohusk" (see Chapter 4). This is a familiar pattern: individual Jews in a village, recorded in the register of the adjacent registration district.

## 2. The Life of the Village Jews on the Bug

### 2.1 The Jews in Świerże — the Documented Data

- The first Jews settled in Świerże **in the years 1537–1550** [8].
- In 1565 the town numbered 210 residents, among them **14 Jewish families**; **most of the shops and taverns (karczmy) in Świerże were in Jewish hands** [8].
- In 1769: **120 Jews** [8].
- The Jewish cemetery: according to the FODZ foundation it was founded **in the second half of the 18th century**, its area about 0.34 hectares [9]; according to Przemysław Burchard (cited in the Polish Wikipedia) — "probably in the 19th century" [10]. Two sources, two estimates — the exact date is **not known**. Only two fragments of gravestones have survived; the cemetery was desecrated by the Germans in World War II [9][10]. The very existence of a cemetery is the strongest evidence that Świerże was the community center of the surrounding area — villages such as Dorohusk and Żmudź almost certainly buried their dead here (a compelling conclusion, not direct documentation).
- In 1900: 480 residents in the osada, of whom **315 were Jews (65.6%)** [8][11].
- On the eve of World War I, the gmina (community) of Świerże was **the richest in the Chełm district after Wojsławice**, and the Jews held the leading positions in trade [8].
- Milestones after the report's period: community rabbi 1910–1918 Josif Mordko Arbuz; 1921: 294 Jews out of 355 residents; kosher slaughtering and a steam bathhouse; a ghetto from 1940; liquidation in 1942 by deportation to Sobibór (directly or via the Włodawa ghetto) [8]. (The sztetl demographic table shows a different figure for 1921 — 40 Jews out of 1,456 — most likely for the entire rural gmina as opposed to the osada; the contradiction is within the source itself) [11].

### 2.3 What the Village Jews Did for a Living — Background and Local Application

**[General background]** The key figure of village Jewry in Congress Poland is the **arendarz** (leaseholder) — the lessee of the estate owner's propination rights (the production and sale of liquor), the tavern keeper of the karczma, the lessee of the estate's mill, brewery, or dairy. Glenn Dynner has shown that even after a series of laws intended to push the Jews out of rural tavern keeping (beginning with the 1812 law that formally forbade them to sell alcohol in the villages), most rural taverns in the Kingdom of Poland remained in practice under Jewish management — through straw arrangements, fictitious Christian "partners," and licenses in the names of peasants — because the estate owners depended on propination revenues and on the Jew who knew how to run it [12]. Alongside the tavern keepers, the villages were home to petty grain merchants, peddlers, tailors, and craftsmen — and at the bottom of the ladder **hired laborers (robotnicy)**, like Moszko-Hersz Szyfman (Chapter 4).

In Świerże itself the pattern is documented directly as early as the 16th century: "most of the shops and karczmy were in Jewish hands" [8], and in the 19th century the place had exactly the facilities around which village Jews earned their living — a liquor distillery, mills, a river harbor [1]. On the specific occupations of the Jews of Dorohusk and Żmudź — **no direct documentation has been located**.

### 2.4 The Bug as a Trade Artery

Documented locally: a large river harbor operated in Świerże thanks to its location on the Bug [1].

**[General background]** Until the coming of the railways (which reached the Chełm area only in 1877, with the Vistula line, the Nadwiślańska) the Bug was the region's only cheap transport route: grain, timber, and forest products were floated downstream on rafts (spław) and barges to the Narew and the Vistula, and from there to Gdańsk. The floating was seasonal — mainly during the spring flood — and employed an entire chain: grain merchants who bought from the estates, agents who organized the rafts, and raftsmen. Jews played a central role in the trade and brokerage links of this chain throughout the Vistula basin. It must be said honestly: name-level documentation of a Jew from Świerże or Dorohusk engaged in the river trade — **has not been located**; the connection between the documented harbor and the livelihood of the local Jews is a reasonable inference, not a documented fact. After 1815 the Bug in this section also became an administrative border between Congress Poland and the Volhynia Governorate — the opposite bank was the "Russia" of the Pale of Settlement, with all the differences of law and customs that entailed.

### 2.5 Relations with the Peasants

**[General background]** The village population on this bank of the Bug was mostly Ukrainian-Ruthenian peasants ("Uniates" until the abolition of the Uniate Church in the Chełm region in 1875, forcibly Orthodox thereafter) alongside a Polish-Catholic minority — and among them individual Jewish families. In the research literature (Dynner and others) a relationship of daily mutual dependence emerges: the Jewish tavern keeper extended credit to the peasant, bought his surplus grain, and the karczma served as the center of village life; alongside this stood the ever-present gaps of religion and class, and the ferment around propination debts. Specific testimony on Jewish–peasant relations in Świerże, Dorohusk, or Żmudź — **has not been located**. It is worth noting that unlike nearby Wojsławice (the blood libel of 1761), no documentation of anti-Jewish violence in these villages during the report's period has been located.

## 3. The Świerże Registration District (okręg bożniczy)

### 3.1 What Is a "Synagogue District"

**[General background]** In Congress Poland the kahals (kahały) were abolished in 1821/1822, and in their place "synagogue supervisory boards" (dozory bóżnicze) were established — religious committees under government supervision, each of which served a "synagogue district" (okręg bóżniczy): a center town and all the villages around it [13]. From 1826 the registration of the Jewish population was separated from the general registration: in each such district a registrar kept the books of births, marriages, and deaths of **all the Jews of the district** — including those who lived in villages without any Jewish institution. Therefore a Jew who was born, married, or died in Dorohusk or in Żmudź appears in the books of Świerże: the address in the body of the act is the village, but the registration — in the center town.

### 3.2 The Świerże District — Documented

- The State Archive in Lublin (APL) holds fond no. **35/1774/0: "Akta stanu cywilnego Okręgu Bożniczego w Świerżach"** — acts of the Jewish civil registration of the Świerże district [14]. The existence of a separate fond is the formal proof that Świerże — a settlement of a few hundred residents — was an independent Jewish registration district, and was not attached to Chełm.
- The composition of the district (exactly which villages belonged to it) is not detailed in any accessible source that has been located, but the acts themselves prove it from within: **death act 6/1833 of Dwora Szyfman née Bukszpan, a resident of Dorohusk, was registered in Świerże** (Chapter 4) — meaning Dorohusk belonged to the Świerże registration district. The record of Moszek Szyfman from the "village of Żmudź/Dorohusk" (1832) in the same series of acts indicates that Żmudź too — or at least its Jews connected to the family — was registered in Świerże.
- Jewish records from the period 1810–1825 (the Duchy of Warsaw and the early Kingdom) were kept in the general civil register, shared by Christians and Jews, by parish [15] — and therefore the search for the generation of Aron and Perla Bukszpan (before 1826) should be conducted in the civil act books of the Świerże and Dorohusk parishes, not in the Jewish books.
- An indirect measure of prosperity: as noted, on the eve of World War I the gmina of Świerże ranked second in wealth in the Chełm district [8] — a tiny community that maintained its own rabbi, kosher slaughtering, a bathhouse, and a cemetery.

### 4.1 Why They Left the Village for the Town — Explaining the Migration to Wojsławice

**[General background]** The migration of Moszko-Hersz Szyfman from Świerże to Wojsławice is a particular case of one of the great processes of Polish Jewry in the 19th century — the emptying of the Jewish village into the town:

1. **Legislation against rural tavern keeping** — a series of laws and tax decrees (from 1812 onward, and especially the increase in license fees in the 1840s) explicitly intended to sever the Jews from selling alcohol in the villages, which steadily eroded the central rural livelihood [12].
2. **Revocation of town rights from tiny towns** (Świerże — 1822 [1][2]) pushed commercial activity toward the towns that remained centers of markets and fairs — Wojsławice, with its fairs and market, versus dwindling Świerże.
3. **The abolition of serfdom (1864)** changed the estate economy: the freed peasant needed less brokerage, and estate owners reduced leaseholds.
4. **Demography** — rapid Jewish natural increase could not be absorbed by rigid village livelihoods; the younger sons went down to the town as laborers, apprentices, and peddlers.
5. **The pull of the community** — only in the town were there a regular minyan, a cheder, a mikveh, and a matchmaking market.

Wojsławice the absorber: in 1827 it was home to 345 Jews (22.3% of the population) [16], and the community grew steadily through the 19th century to 1,235 persons in 1928 [16] — growth part of which is precisely families like Szyfman, who came down from the Bug villages.
