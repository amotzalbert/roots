# From Occupied Poland to the Soviet Union and Back: The Historical Background to the Path Taken by Jewish Refugees, 1939–1951

**Methodological note:** This document presents general, documented, and sourced historical background on the phenomenon of Jews fleeing occupied Poland to the Soviet Union in 1939–1941 and their journeys up to aliyah (immigration) to Israel. It is **not** a document verifying specific details about the Schafir-Moskal family. We have no archival sources on the private route taken by David Moskal, Malka Schafir, or members of their families — it is only known (from the family) that they fled eastward at the beginning of the war, survived, and immigrated to Israel in the early 1950s, after they had already married. Every detail referring to the family itself is explicitly marked as such; everything else is general historical context applying to tens of thousands of Jewish families who followed a similar route.

---

## 1. The Flight Eastward, September 1939 – June 1941

With Germany's invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939 and the Soviet Union's invasion from the east on 17 September (under the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact), Poland was split into two occupation zones. The dividing line ran roughly along the Narew, Vistula, and San rivers. Hundreds of thousands of residents — Jews and non-Jews alike — suddenly found themselves on the seam line between two occupiers, and many chose to cross eastward into the territory annexed to the Soviet Union.

**Numbers and scale:** According to the accepted scholarly estimate, about 300,000 Polish Jews — nearly 10% of all Polish Jewry at the time — fled from the territories occupied by Germany into the Soviet zone between September 1939 and June 1941 (Yad Vashem / USHMM). Some did so within days or weeks of the outbreak of the war, and some continued crossing the border during 1940, at times illegally, at the risk of arrest.

**Motives:** The flight eastward was not an organized movement but a series of individual and family decisions, driven mainly by:
- Immediate fear of German occupation — reports (some accurate, some rumors) of Nazi violence against Jews already in the first weeks of the occupation.
- Geographic proximity: settlements in eastern Poland (such as the area around Chełm, Wojsławice, Rawa Ruska) were physically close to the dividing line, which made the crossing practical for entire families.
- Ideological considerations among a minority of the refugees — sympathy for communism or hope for a better life under Soviet rule, mainly among the young.
- Many saw it as a temporary move — they expected to return home when the situation stabilized, and did not plan permanent emigration.

**Routes:** The crossing was usually made on foot or in wagons, through forests and fields near the rural borders, sometimes with the help of local smugglers, sometimes under the watch (and sometimes under the fire) of border guards on both sides. Many families were separated during the flight — some family members crossed and remained in the east, others stayed behind in the German occupation zone, often with no practical possibility of reuniting.

Key source: [German Invasion of Poland: Jewish Refugees, 1939 — USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-invasion-of-poland-jewish-refugees-1939)

## 2. The Fate of the Refugees in the Soviet Union: Citizenship, Deportation, "Unreliability"

The refugees who reached the territory annexed to the Soviet Union (western Ukraine and western Belarus) faced a dilemma imposed on them from above: the Soviet authorities demanded that they accept Soviet citizenship. Those who refused — out of loyalty to Poland, hope of returning home, or fear of commitment to the communist regime — were classified as politically "unreliable" (*neblagonadyozhny*, неблагонадёжный).

**The Soviet deportation, June 1940:** In the largest deportation wave of Jewish refugees, in June 1940, the NKVD (the Soviet secret police) deported tens of thousands of Jewish refugees — mainly those who had refused to accept Soviet citizenship — to forced-labor camps (the Gulag), restricted-exile settlements ("ссылка"), and exile villages in Siberia, Kazakhstan, and Central Asia. Scholarly estimates (among them the pioneering work of Yosef Litvak, *Jewish Refugees from Poland in the Soviet Union, 1939–1946*, The Hebrew University, 1988) speak of tens of thousands of deportees out of all the Jewish refugees; the total number of Jewish and Polish refugees together deported in the various waves (1940–1941) is estimated in the hundreds of thousands.

The conditions of the deportation and of life after it were extremely harsh: forced labor, malnutrition, extreme cold, disease — and high mortality, especially among children and the elderly. Nevertheless, and by a paradox of history, it was precisely this deportation — experienced at the time as a catastrophe — that saved most of the deportees from extermination: they were beyond the reach of the Nazis when Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941 ("Operation Barbarossa"), and the systematic extermination of the Jews of Eastern Europe began.

**Those who remained in the annexed territories:** Jewish refugees who did accept Soviet citizenship, or who were not deported for some other reason, remained in the cities and towns of western Ukraine and western Belarus (Lwów, Brześć, Białystok, and others) under Soviet rule. Some managed to integrate economically and socially; but when the Germans invaded in June 1941, these areas were overrun very quickly (unlike Barbarossa's application to the Soviet interior), many of them did not manage to flee eastward in time, and fell victim to the Nazi extermination together with the local Jews. In other words — those sent eastward as "unreliable" in 1940 mostly survived; those who remained in territory reoccupied by the Germans in 1941 faced a far higher risk.

Sources: [Exile of Jews in the Soviet interior during World War II — Wikipedia (based on academic research)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exile_of_Jews_in_the_Soviet_interior_during_World_War_II); [The Distress of Jews in the Soviet Union — Yad Vashem Studies (Altshuler)](https://www.yadvashem.org/yv/pdf-drupal/en/research/yv-studies/altshuler.pdf); Yosef Litvak, *Pelitim Yehudim mi-Polin be-Berit ha-Mo'atsot, 1939–1946* (Hebrew University, Jerusalem, 1988); Katherine Jolluck and others, summarized in [Polish Jews in the Soviet Union (1939–1959): History and Memory of Deportation, Exile, and Survival](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv26rr9xg).

## 3. The Sikorski-Mayski Agreement and the Amnesty, 1941

On 30 July 1941, about a month after Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union, an agreement was signed in London between the prime minister of the Polish government-in-exile, Władysław Sikorski, and the Soviet ambassador to Britain, Ivan Mayski (the Sikorski–Mayski Agreement). The agreement renewed diplomatic relations between the Polish government-in-exile and the Soviet Union — relations that had been severed with the Soviet invasion of 1939 — and included a Soviet commitment to an "amnesty" (амнистия) for all Polish citizens held in detention, captivity, or exile within the territory of the Soviet Union.

On 12 August 1941 the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR issued the actual "amnesty" decree. The decree applied, according to scholarly estimates, to about 1.25 million Polish citizens (Jews and non-Jews) held in labor camps, internal exile, or imprisonment. For them, the amnesty meant legal freedom of movement within the Soviet Union for the first time since the deportation — but not necessarily means, employment, or a roof over their heads.

In the wake of the agreement, a free Polish army was formed on Soviet territory under the command of General Władysław Anders ("Anders' Army"), which included Jewish volunteers as well. A significant portion of the Jews released under the amnesty tried to move south, toward warmer regions in Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyzstan — sometimes following the movement of Anders' Army, sometimes independently — with the aim of improving their chances of survival. Living conditions in Central Asia were still extremely harsh (hunger, diseases such as typhus and intestinal infections, refugee pressure on meager local infrastructure), but the geographic opening and the exit from the forced-labor camps improved the chances of survival for many.

Sources: [Sikorski–Mayski agreement — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikorski%E2%80%93Mayski_agreement); [Amnesty for Polish citizens in the Soviet Union — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amnesty_for_Polish_citizens_in_the_Soviet_Union); [The Sikorski-Maisky Agreement: a tactical success but a strategic defeat — Polish History (instytutpileckiego.pl)](https://polishhistory.pl/the-sikorski-maisky-agreement-a-tactical-success-but-a-strategic-defeat/).

## 4. The Repatriation, 1945–1946

Toward the end of the war and after it, a formal agreement was signed between the Polish provisional government (the pro-Soviet one, established in Lublin) and the Soviet Union. On 6 July 1945 a repatriation agreement was signed granting "persons of Polish and Jewish nationality" (the agreement's wording) the right to renounce the Soviet citizenship previously forced upon them and to move ("be repatriated") to Poland.

**Scale of the return:** By mid-1946 about 137,000 repatriates had returned to Poland under the agreement, and in a further wave in July–August 1946 about 140,000 more Polish Jews returned — so that the total repatriation of Polish Jews from the Soviet Union to Poland between 1945 and 1946 is estimated at about 200,000–250,000 persons (the exact figures vary between research sources, depending on definitions and the measurement period). This was the overwhelming majority of Polish Jews who survived the war: of the roughly 3.3 million Jews who lived in Poland in 1939, only about 350,000 survived — and most of these survivors (between 190,000 and 220,000, according to estimates) survived thanks to refuge in the Soviet Union, not inside occupied Poland or in the concentration and extermination camps.

The trains carrying the repatriates usually crossed through border stations such as Medyka and other stations on Poland's new eastern border. The journey took weeks and sometimes months, in conditions of severe overcrowding, mostly in freight cars. Those who reached Poland found a country destroyed — economically and physically — and often the family home occupied by non-Jewish neighbors, and no surviving relatives.

**Important for our purposes:** The repatriation to Poland in 1945–1946 was usually only a transit station, not a final destination. The wave of antisemitic violence that erupted in postwar Poland — above all the Kielce pogrom of 4 July 1946, in which about 42 Jews were murdered — convinced many of the returnees that they had no future in Poland, and accelerated further movement westward, to the displaced-persons camps in Germany, Austria, and Italy.

Sources: [Repatriation (1944–47) — Kresy-Siberia Foundation](https://kresy-siberia.org/museum-galleries/repatriation-1944-47/); [Polish–Soviet border agreement of August 1945 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Soviet_border_agreement_of_August_1945); [Polish-Jewish Refugees Repatriated from the Soviet Union at the End of the Second World War and Afterwards — Springer / Palgrave chapter, based on the research of Yosef Litvak](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-349-21789-2_13); [Those Who Stayed: Polish Jews Who Remained in the Soviet Union after the Second World War — Holocaust and Genocide Studies, Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/hgs/article/39/3/507/8305306).

## 5. The Road to Israel: Bricha, DP Camps, and Aliyah 1946–1951

**The Bricha:** The "Bricha" ("flight/escape") movement — an unofficial but tightly knit organization led by surviving Jewish underground fighters, partisans, and Zionist youth movements (among them Abba Kovner) — was formally established in Lublin in January 1945 and operated until 1948. Its goal was to move Jews from Poland (and later also from Romania, Hungary, and the Soviet Union) westward, through Czechoslovakia, Hungary, and Austria, to the Allied-controlled zones in Germany and Italy — from where one could try to reach the Land of Israel, usually illegally ("Ha'apala", also known as Aliyah Bet).

According to the accepted estimates, about 250,000 Jews passed through the Bricha between 1945 and 1948, with the peak of activity in the years 1945–1946 (about 180,000 of them). Most of the participants were Holocaust survivors who had returned from the Soviet Union in the repatriation and found no future in postwar communist Poland — and not concentration-camp survivors returning directly from Germany, who were a relative minority of all the Jews who survived.

**The displaced-persons camps (DP camps):** Those who reached Germany, Austria, or Italy were usually directed to displaced-persons camps operated by Allied forces with the assistance of Jewish organizations, foremost among them the "Joint" (American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee), which financed a considerable part of the transit and subsistence costs. In camps such as Landsberg, Feldafing, and Burghausen in Germany, and many others, Jews waited months and sometimes years while aliyah to the Land of Israel was being organized — legal (within the British Mandate quotas, which were very limited) or illegal ("Ha'apala" on clandestine immigrant ships).

**Aliyah 1946–1951:** Most of the Jews of the DP camps did not manage to immigrate before the establishment of the state, owing to the British policy restricting immigration to the Land of Israel (the "White Paper"). Only after the Declaration of Independence in May 1948 and the lifting of the restrictions did the way open to legal, mass immigration. Between 1948 and 1951 about 700,000 immigrants came to Israel in total (the state's first great mass immigration), among them a considerable number of Holocaust survivors and refugees who had survived the war in the Soviet Union, gone through the repatriation route to Poland, and then the Bricha to Western Europe and the DP camps — or who immigrated directly from communist Poland under limited emigration agreements approved between Israel and Poland in 1949–1950.
