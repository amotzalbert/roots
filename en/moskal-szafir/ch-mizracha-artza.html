Moskal–Schafir · Chapter 9

Eastward, and from there to the Land of Israel

David Moskal and Malka Schafir, who were already married, fled eastward into Soviet territory before the German occupation reached Wojsławice, and survived. That is all the family knows about their own particular route. This chapter tells the road travelled by tens of thousands of families in a similar position — in order to give that short sentence a body.

The flight eastward, 1939–1941

With the German invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939, and the Soviet invasion from the east on the 17th of that month under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, Poland was cut into two zones of occupation. Some three hundred thousand Polish Jews — almost a tenth of all Polish Jewry — fled from the territories occupied by Germany into the area annexed by the Soviet Union, between September 1939 and June 19411. Localities in eastern Poland, such as the Chełm region and its surroundings, were physically close to the dividing line — which made the crossing practical for whole families. The crossing itself was made mostly on foot or by cart, through forests and fields, at times with the help of smugglers, and at times under fire from border guards1.

The fate in the Soviet Union

The refugees who reached the territory annexed by the Soviet Union faced a Soviet demand to take citizenship. Those who refused were classed as politically "unreliable"; in June 1940 the NKVD deported tens of thousands of them to labour camps, special settlements and villages of exile in Siberia, in Kazakhstan and in Central Asia2. The conditions of the deportation were very harsh — forced labour, cold, hunger, high mortality among children and the elderly — but in a historical paradox, it was precisely this deportation that put most of them beyond the reach of the Nazis when Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941. Those who remained in the annexed territories and were not deported, by contrast, stood at far greater risk when the German occupation reached the region.

The amnesty, 1941

On 30 July 1941 the Sikorski–Mayski agreement was signed between the Polish government in exile and the Soviet Union, and in its wake an "amnesty" was declared for Polish citizens held in exile or in prison — some one and a quarter million people. Many of the Jews who were released tried to move south, towards warmer regions in Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan, to improve their chances of survival, although conditions of life there — hunger, disease, pressure on meagre infrastructure — remained hard3.

The repatriation, 1945–1946

After the war an agreement between Poland and the Soviet Union allowed Polish Jews to renounce the Soviet citizenship that had been imposed on them and to return to Poland. Some two hundred to two hundred and fifty thousand Polish Jews returned in this way between 1945 and 19464. This is the overwhelming majority of the Polish Jews who survived the war: out of about three million three hundred thousand who lived in Poland in 1939, only some three hundred and fifty thousand survived — and most of those survivors survived thanks to the refuge in the Soviet Union, not inside occupied Poland. Those who returned to Poland found a ruined country, and at times a house occupied by neighbours; the Kielce pogrom, on 4 July 1946, in which about forty-two Jews were murdered, convinced many of the returnees that they had no future in Poland4.

The flight westward, and from there to the Land of Israel

The "Bricha" movement — an unofficial organisation led by survivors of the Jewish undergrounds and partisan units and by Zionist youth movements — was formally established in Lublin in January 1945, and moved some quarter of a million Jews from Poland westward, through Czechoslovakia, Hungary and Austria, to camps under Allied control in Germany and Italy5. In the displaced persons camps many waited months and years, while illegal immigration to the Land of Israel contended with the British restrictions on immigration. Only after the declaration of independence in May 1948 did the way open for lawful mass immigration — some seven hundred thousand immigrants between 1948 and 1951, among them survivors and refugees who had survived the war in the Soviet Union and passed along this route, or who came directly from Poland under limited emigration agreements in 1949–19505.

Wrocław, and from there to the Land of Israel

The registration cards from Wrocław, March 1947, place David and Malka in Lower Silesia — the region to which the Polish repatriation office directed the returnees, in territory annexed from Germany. Both are recorded as having survived in the Soviet Union, and both are working: he in a manufacturing plant, she as a labourer9. Ten years later, in January 1957, Malka is already filling in Pages of Testimony in Petah Tikva.

Exactly when they emigrated, between March 1947 and January 1957, is not documented. The documented emigration windows from Poland narrow the possibilities: a wave that closed in September 1950, followed by an almost complete closure, and then the wave of 1956–1957. Both windows are open, and there is no document that decides between them.
