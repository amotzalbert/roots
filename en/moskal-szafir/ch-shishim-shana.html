Moskal–Schafir · Chapter 5

«Sixty Years of My Life in Wojsławice»: Mendel Schafir

The son of a poor roofer who made his living from peasants' roofs wrote, in his old age, the most detailed inside account that survives of life in Wojsławice. This memoir is the anchor of the entire branch — it is what established Szmul Schafir's name, his trade, and the family story that could not have been reconstructed from any act — and which the acts, once found, confirmed.

Principal source: Mendel Schafir, «Sixty Years of My Life in Wojsławice, 1879–1939» Sefer Zikaron Wojsławice, Tel Aviv 1970, pp. 69–95

Who wrote it, and what remains open

Menachem Mendel Schafir, son of Szmul Schafir and Pesia-Ruchla Szyfman, wrote his memoir in Yiddish; it was translated into Hebrew and printed in the community's memorial book in 19701. His birth was registered late, in 1890, and refers to a birth on 3 January 1887; the title of his own memoir — "1879–1939" — points to a birth eight years earlier, and so does its opening ("in the summer I turned three… the eighteen-eighties"). Both dates are given here side by side, without a ruling2. His mother's name, Pesia-Ruchla, is not mentioned in the memoir even once by her given name — it always opens with "my father", never with "my mother" by name.

His father, who was never called by name — until the peasants called him

The previous chapter has already given the moment in which peasants called his father "Shmuelke" in cries of joy. From the memoir the picture of his life also emerges: Szmul Schafir roofed houses — straw for the poor, tiles for the nobility — and wandered from village to village, at times taking no payment. He would live a whole week on black bread and onion, and in a nobleman's yard he would drink milk straight from the udder. His name carried as far as Chełm and Lublin, and noblemen who sent him to repair tiled roofs recommended him to their friends1.

«My father belonged to Bełz Hasidism and believed greatly in the Rebbe of Bełz. He did indeed instruct him to marry my mother, after he had earlier taken her elder sister to wife and she died on him without bearing children. There remained only a sister of fifteen who was to grant him halitzah. […] The Rebbe told him not to accept halitzah, but to marry her. Father was already in his forties by then; Mother bore him four sons and four daughters.» Mendel Schafir, ibid., pp. 74–75 (Hebrew edition).

This passage was put to the test of the registers, and passed it. Death act 7/1872 documents that Estera-Dwojra Szyfman died on 30 May 1872, aged twenty-two, with no recorded children; eight months later, on 21 January 1873, the widower married her seventeen-year-old sister, and both acts name the same parents for the two of them3. The memory is accurate in the shape of the story and mistaken in the ages: Mendel wrote "in his forties" and "aged fifteen", and the acts give thirty-six and seventeen. And one detail the memoir omits is precisely what explains the need for the Rebbe's ruling: Szmul was a widower, and not only of Estera-Dwojra — before her, in 1859, he had married Sura Szrojd of Dubienka, a wife the memoir does not mention at all3.

The arrest, quietly

Szmul Schafir was arrested for refusing to send his son to the Russian government school; the commander warned him that a further refusal meant a full year's imprisonment. Mendel heard him, imprisoned because of his son, reciting Psalms in the cell.

«Father listened in silence and did not answer. He had no thought of giving in at all.» Mendel Schafir, ibid.

The countess and half a mitzvah

After a fire in the town, the widowed countess rebuilt the houses of the poor, and Szmul Schafir roofed them with straw. She still owed him a few roubles; he waived them — "it will be all right" — because his heart would not let him press her after she had already spent so much. She was furious: "Aha, I see you want to trick me out of half a mitzvah — you will not succeed", and she paid him to the last penny1.

Herszl, the eldest brother

Mendel's eldest brother, Herszl (Hersz) Schafir, took part in the self-defence of 1905 — among the young men who surrounded and drove off rioters who tried to start a riot in the market on market day. Herszl married and settled in Rejowiec; Mendel used to travel to him for the Sabbath.

"In our house sons and daughters were born and grew up who drew sap and marrow from its roots and branched out like oaks, drawn upwards to the sun, but never reached it. We all worked hard all our lives to bring a living into the house, and we never had the chance to sate the soul with the little knowledge we so very often longed for." That is how Mendel describes his parents' house — a family of roofers that longed for learning.

Mendel and Nachman Syrkin

Still a boy, he was working with his father in a village near Chełm, and came into the town for the afternoon and evening prayers. After the prayers he heard a heated argument about a Jewish student who had arrived from Warsaw a few days earlier — Nachman Syrkin, founder of socialist Zionism, who spoke Yiddish and delivered a Zionist sermon. The beit midrash nearly came to blows between his admirers and his opponents; Mendel carried the news back to Wojsławice, and all through the Sabbath the town spoke of nothing else. More than thirty years later his daughter Dvora ran the Po'alei Zion evening classes in the town itself4.

The epidemic and the wedding in the cemetery

Mendel, by then thirty-six, describes an epidemic that broke out in the town under the first German occupation, and the belief that a wedding in the cemetery would halt it — which is indeed what was done: an old spinster and a widower were found, and the wedding canopy was set up among the graves. "Afterwards the epidemic did diminish, and it was plainly seen that the canopy in the cemetery had indeed helped." His father, Mendel relates, had already met a similar belief years earlier, when he was forbidden to enter Lublin during a cholera epidemic and heard of "a wedding in the cemetery in Lublin, which stopped the cholera."1
