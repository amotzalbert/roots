Moskal–Schafir · Chapter 8

The Destruction

The young fled eastward. The parents and the sisters stayed. Nine of the closest family members of David Moskal and Malka Schafir were murdered or fell in 1942 — all of them documented by name, most of them in Pages of Testimony filled in by Malka herself, in her own hand.

● Documented — twelve Pages of Testimony at Yad Vashem, in the handwriting of Malka Moskal

Who left and who stayed

Wojsławice first fell into the hands of the Soviet army in October 1939, and the Germans came in their place about two weeks later1. In that short window it was still possible to leave eastward, and the younger generation left. The parents did not. This is the sharpest division in the entire file: those who were young and able to move — survived; those who were older, married, tied to a house or a business — stayed, and were murdered.

The Schafir house: a mother and two sons

Perl (Pnina) Schafir née Liben, wife of Menachem Mendel and mother of Malka, born in Włodzimierz Wołyński, aged fifty-four — murdered in the Chełm ghetto in 1942. Her daughter Malka filled in a Page of Testimony for her in Petah Tikva, on 7 January 1957, and signed on the line for the relationship: "daughter"2.

Two of Malka's brothers went east, reached Kostroma in Russia — and both fell in 1942, in Soviet uniform:

WhoThe details
Shmuel Schafir Aged 21, a carpenter, unmarried. Fell on 1 January 1942. Malka wrote on the form, in her own words: «Killed while fighting the Nazis, in the ranks of the Red Army»3
Shalom Schafir Aged 30, married to Rivka née Ejdelman. Fell in battle in 1942, he too at Kostroma3

1942 took the mother in Poland and the two sons in Russia — the same year, the same enemy, three places.

The Moskal house: a mother and four daughters

On David Moskal's side, the entire household that remained in the town perished. His mother and his four sisters — David was the only son, and had no brothers4:

Lea, and the Borochov Library

The Wojsławice memorial book lists, in the chapter on the parties and organisations, the names of the eight young people who founded the Borochov Library in the town in 1922 — the Po'alei Zion library, named after Ber Borochov. In the list, between Neta Handelsman and Lejbl Hochlerer, appears "Lahtshe Moskal"6. At the first meeting each of them contributed a sum of money, and with the money the first books were bought; for a time they were kept in the flat of Szewa Klang. In another chapter the author of the memoir lists "the girls who came to the library and devoured the books", and Lahtshe Moskal is among them.

And in the same town, in exactly the same circle: Dvora Schafir, Malka's sister, ran the Po'alei Zion Yiddish evening classes and took part in the drama circle8. Mendel Schafir's daughter and Lejb Moskal's daughter belonged to the same Zionist-socialist circle in one small town — and this, apparently, is how David Moskal and Malka Schafir came to know each other.

Tovaleh, who came back from Sobibór

A survivor's testimony printed in the memorial book tells of a day on which the Germans entered the houses and took people for forced labour:

«One day they came into our house and took my brother Yechiel. They took many Jews in the town then, Michle Turbiner, Tovaleh Moskal, Szije Lang and many others, who were sent to Sobibór for hard labour. Michle and Tovaleh came back.» A survivor's testimony, Sefer Zikaron Wojsławice, Tel Aviv 1970, Hebrew edition p. 258.

Tovaleh — Tauba Moskal, David's youngest sister, aged twenty — was taken captive for forced labour at Sobibór, survived it, and returned to Wojsławice. And then she was murdered anyway, in 1942, as her sister-in-law Malka would record on a Page of Testimony fifty years later7.

The community

The German army took Wojsławice on 11 September 1939. An open ghetto was established; the synagogue was converted into stables. In October 1942 the ghetto was liquidated and all the Jews of the town were marched out and deported to Sobibór — according to one source via Chełm, according to another via Włodawa. In that same month sixty Jews caught in hiding places were shot in the meadows north of the market square9. The Sobibór extermination camp, to which they were taken, operated from the spring of 1942 until October 1943, and according to the cautious estimate of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum at least one hundred and sixty-seven thousand people were murdered there10.

And in the necrology of the memorial book, in the list of the community's victims compiled by the people of the town, three women of the Moskal house appear side by side: Dvora, Sitshe and Tovaleh11 — the mother and two of her daughters, the same names Malka wrote on the Pages of Testimony.
