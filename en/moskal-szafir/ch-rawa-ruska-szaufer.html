Moskal–Schafir · Chapter 3

From Galicia: Rawa Ruska and the Schafir house

A second branch, from an entirely different place: not from the Russian villages of the Bug, but from a border town in Austrian Galicia, a day's journey from the court of the Rebbe of Bełz. From there came Chaim Schafir, who crossed the border into Wojsławice some time before 1838.

◐ Probable — the identification of the place depends on the clerk's reading, not on a separate act Sources: death act 9/1866 · Rawa Ruska yizkor book (1973)

A border town under two rulers

Rawa Ruska, some fifty kilometres north-west of Lwów, passed in 1772, in the first partition of Poland, to Austrian rule as part of the "Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria"1. From 1815 the Congress of Vienna marked a fixed border between it and Russian "Congress Poland" to its north — so that Rawa Ruska itself became a border town, and on the other side of that border, about seventy-five kilometres away as the crow flies, stood Wojsławice.

Chaim Schafir — born in Galicia, living in Russian Poland

Chaim Schafir (Chaim Szaufer) was born around 1804. The only source for this is his death act, registered in Wojsławice in 1866, which names his place of birth explicitly: "in the town of Rawa in Austrian Galicia"2. The identification with Rawa Ruska — and not with Rawa Mazowiecka, another town of the same name which lies precisely in Russian Poland — is no more than probable: the clerk noted "Austrian Galicia" explicitly, which rules out the Russian Rawa, but does not establish absolute certainty. His parents, Szmul and Estera Szaufer, are mentioned in the same act as already deceased ("niegdy" — the late), and no direct record of the place or date of their deaths has been found.

The recording of a place of birth in an act is, in itself, a finding of weight: a registrar does not invent such a detail, and it almost always reflects a statement by the family itself. Decades after he settled in Wojsławice, he — or whoever testified for him — still knew and stated his Galician origin2.

Szmul Szaufer ⚭ Estera Szaufer — named in their son's death act; died before 1866
Chaim Szaufer ⚭ Liba — he b. ~1804 in Rawa, d. 3.7.1866; she b. ~1810 in Wojsławice, d. 13.7.1874

His life in Wojsławice, as the acts show it

Chaim Schafir does not disappear between the two acts. He appears in them across twenty-eight years, and each time with one more detail:

ActWhat it says about him
birth 15/1838
(9.10.1838)		 "Chaim Szaufer, kotlar [coppersmith ], resident of Wojsławice, aged thirty-three" — registering his son Lejb, born on 1 October. His wife Liba, aged twenty-eight. The act is signed in his own hand, in Latin letters and beside them in Hebrew6
death 9/1866
(3.7.1866)		 "house owner (właściciel domu), resident of Wojsławice, aged sixty-two, son of the late Szmul and Estera, born in the town of Rawa in Austrian Galicia", leaving a widow — Liba2

The two ages cross-check: aged 33 in October 1838 gives a birth around 1805, and aged 62 in July 1866 gives around 1804 — a difference of one year between two documents written twenty-eight years apart. The year of birth holds. And the migration from Galicia is not a reconstruction: on 9 October 1838 he is already a "resident of Wojsławice", with a trade and a child, and aged thirty-three6.

Chaim Schafir could sign his name — in two alphabets. His son Szmul, two generations later in the same town, could not: in the acts of 1859 and 1878 it is recorded explicitly that he did not know how to sign. Literacy does not always rise with the generations.

Liba, and the maiden name that is not resolved

Chaim's wife, Liba, was born around 1810 in Wojsławice itself, and died there on 13 July 1874 at the age of sixty-four, a widow7. Over her maiden name there is a contradiction that has not been resolved: three acts call her née Kielner (her son's marriage 1859, her husband's death 1866, her son's marriage 1873), and two call her née Glazer (her son's marriage 31.12.1872, and her own death act in 1874, which even names her parents: Herszek and Masza Glazer)7.

One argument tips the balance towards Glazer: the witness at her son's birth act in 1838 was "Herszek Glazer the glazier, aged fifty" — that is, born around 1788, a gap of twenty-two years from her. At her son Gersz's marriage in 1872 the witness was likewise Gersz Glazer the glazier, aged forty-six. The Glazer family is present at the family's events across thirty-four years. The explanation that reconciles both names without accusing anyone of error — that Liba née Glazer had earlier been the widow or the divorcee of a Kielner — is a possibility, not a conclusion7.

Why people left Galicia

Migration from Galicia to Congress Poland in the first decades of the nineteenth century is a phenomenon documented in the genealogical literature, and it rests on a chain of decrees imposed on the Jews of Galicia alone, which did not exist on the Russian side of the border: the candle tax and the kosher meat tax, which required payment for the very observance of the commandments; a marriage tax and at times also a compulsory examination in German, without which a marriage was not recognised as lawful; and conscription into the Austrian army from 1788, while the Jews of Congress Poland were exempt from service in exchange for a tax until 18433. Chaim Schafir, about twenty-five years old around 1829, was exactly at the age at which such considerations — marriage, conscription — become urgent. Exactly when he crossed the border, and what his immediate reason was — not known; he is already documented as living in Wojsławice by the 1830s at the latest4.

Seventy-five kilometres as the crow flies, and yet a passage between two empires: until 1772 this was a single Jewish living space, with no border. The line Chaim Schafir crossed as a young man had been drawn on the map only a generation before him.

The Hasidic surroundings

In about 1817 R. Shalom Rokeach established his court in Bełz, some twenty-five kilometres from Rawa Ruska, and Bełz became the Hasidic centre of the whole of Galicia5. A family living in Rawa Ruska in the generation of Szmul and Estera Szaufer therefore lived within a day's journey of the court of Bełz at its height. This is not enough to establish that the family itself was Bełz Hasidic — no such affiliation is documented — but this is the religious context from which Chaim Schafir came, and it is also the context to which the family returned, by an entirely different route, when his son Szmul ("Shmuelke") Schafir became a Bełz Hasid in Wojsławice itself — as described in the previous chapter.
