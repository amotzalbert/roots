Moskal–Schafir · Chapter 2

On the Banks of the Bug

Dorohusk and Świerże, and from there to Wojsławice: a chain of five generations, documented almost without a gap, from Aron and Perla Bukszpan, born around 1760, to Mendel Schafir, who set the family story down in writing in 1970.

● Largely documented — two death acts read in full Sources: Archiwum Państwowe w Lublinie, fonds 35/1774 and 35/1787

Two small villages on the west bank of the Bug, Świerże and Dorohusk, about eight kilometres apart, in the Chełm district — this is the family's starting point, not Wojsławice itself. Wojsławice, the town to which they eventually moved, lies some thirty kilometres to the south-west. Świerże itself was, until 1822, a proper town, with municipal rights and a large river landing; it then lost its status and became an "osada" — a semi-urban settlement1. In Świerże, in the local Jewish cemetery, the only written record the research managed to locate for the generation that opens the chain has been preserved2.

Aron and Perla Bukszpan

The couple Aron and Perla Bukszpan (Aron i Perla Bukszpanowie), "z Dorohuska" — of Dorohusk — are the deepest generation the research has managed to document across both branches of the family together. Their year of birth is written in no document; it is derived backwards from the age of their daughter at her death: born around 1760–17653. Jewish civil registration in Congress Poland opens only in 1826 — before that, the death or marriage of a Jew is simply not recorded in any surviving register. Aron and Perla Bukszpan lived and died before that boundary, and so their names reach us only as they were written, as indirect evidence, in their daughter's death act.

Dwera Bukszpan, wife of Szyfman

Dwera Szyfman née Bukszpan (Dwera Szyfman z domu Bukszpan) was born in Dorohusk around 17883, and married Josek Szyfman — for whose date of birth no documentation has been found. On 9 October 1833, at three in the afternoon, she died at her home in Dorohusk, aged forty-five. The act was registered in Świerże four days later, on 13 October, on the testimony of Lejb and Szmul Gorman:

«There died Dwera Szyfman, a married woman, resident in the village of Dorohusk, aged forty-five, daughter of Aron and Perla, the spouses Bukszpan of Dorohusk, leaving behind her a widower, her husband Josek Szyfman.» Umarła Dwera Szyfman, Mężatka, we Wsi Dorohusku zamieszkała, lat czterdzieści pięć mająca, córka Arona i Perli Małżonków Bukszpanów z Dorohuska, zostawiwszy po sobie owdowiałego Męża Josia Szyfman. State Archive in Lublin, fond 35/1774/0/1/5 (Świerże — death register 1829–1863), unit 2194412, scan 12 of 13, act 6/1833.

This is the decisive document of the chapter: the single line that anchors every generation above it. Josek Szyfman, her husband, was left a widower — his own year of death is unknown.

The two brothers: Moszek and Moszko-Hersz

Josek and Dwera had at least two sons, both of them recorded in acts that name their parents exactly — evidence beyond doubt that they were brothers. Moszek Szyfman married in Świerże in 1832, aged twenty — that is, born around 1812 — to Rane Katz, daughter of Szloma Kac and Sosia née Laberfarb, whose father was no longer among the living4. The rest of his life — not known.

His younger brother, Moszko Hersz Szyfman, was born not in Dorohusk but in the neighbouring village of Świerże itself, around 1822. He died in Wojsławice on 20 March 1869, and his death act — the first to link the Bug chain to Wojsławice explicitly — was read in full from the scan, not from an index:

«There died, resident in the town of Wojsławice, the Jewish labourer Hersz Szyfman [Moszko Szyfman], aged forty-seven, born in the village of Świerże, the lawful son of Josek and Dwojra née Bukszpan, the spouses Szyfman, leaving behind him a widow, his wife Dwojra Szyfman née Kac.» …умеръ жительствующій въ городѣ Войславицахъ работникъ еврей Гершъ Шифманъ (Moszko Szyfman) сорока семи лѣтъ, урожденный въ деревни Сверже, сынъ Іоса и Двойры урожденной Букшпановой законныхъ супруговъ Шифмановъ, оставивъ послѣ себя вдову жену Дойру Шифманъ урожденную Кацовую. State Archive in Lublin, fond 35/1787/0/2.1/33 (Wojsławice), scan 32 of 41, death act 4/1869.

The act, in Russian — the official language of registration in Congress Poland from 1868 onwards — establishes three things that were not known before: his place of birth (Świerże, not Wojsławice), his occupation (работникъ, "labourer" — the lowest economic rank among the Jews of the village), and his wife's name, Dwojra-Itta née Kac. Moszko-Hersz was, then, the migrant: the first in the chain to leave the villages of the Bug and settle in the town.

Why people leave a village for a town — not a private story but a process. Legislation that barred Jews from the village liquor trade, the stripping of Świerże's municipal rights in 1822, and the abolition of serfdom in 1864 together emptied the village of its livelihood; the town held the market, the minyan and the cheder.

The daughters: Estera-Dwojra and Pesia-Ruchla

Moszko-Hersz and Dwojra-Itta had, among further children who have not been traced, two documented daughters. Estera-Dwojra Szyfman died in Wojsławice on 30 May 1872, aged twenty-two — young, with no known children. Her sister Pesia-Ruchla Szyfman married in Wojsławice on 21 January 1873, aged seventeen — that is, born around 1856 — to Szmul Schafir (Szmul Szaufer), after whom, and after whose history, the whole branch would later carry the family name "Schafir".

Szmul Schafir — "Shmuelke" — and his son's memoir

Szmul Schafir, husband of Pesia-Ruchla, is a figure who rests almost not at all on a single act, but on a full and detailed account: the written memoir of their son, Menachem Mendel Schafir, "Sixty Years of My Life in Wojsławice, 1879–1939", published in 1970 in the Wojsławice memorial book5. His given name is not mentioned explicitly in the memoir even once — it emerges only from the mouths of the peasants of the villages where he worked, who called him by his affectionate name:

«When we came into their huts they burst into cries of joy, shouting and gathering their neighbours: 'Hey, good brothers, Shmuelke has come'.» Mendel Schafir, "Sixty Years of My Life in Wojsławice", in: Shimon Kanc (ed.), Sefer Zikaron Wojsławice, Tel Aviv 1970, pp. 69–95 (Hebrew edition pp. 23–45).

Szmul Schafir's occupation is documented in the acts themselves as кровельщик — a tiler, a roofer . He married three wives, and their order in the acts is fixed8:

And what the acts confirmed in the story of the Rebbe of Bełz

Mendel Schafir wrote in his memoir that his father was a Bełz Hasid, and that the Rebbe of Bełz instructed him to marry his mother — the younger sister of the wife who had died childless6. Every element of that story now stands in the registers: Estera-Dwojra died on 30 May 1872, aged twenty-two, and no children were recorded for her; eight months later, on 21 January 1873, the thirty-six-year-old widower married her seventeen-year-old sister. Both acts name the same parents for the two of them — Dwojra-Itta née Kac, and the father "Berek" (1873) or "Gerszko" (1872), two halves of a double name, apparently Berek-Hersz8.

The memoir is not accurate on ages — Mendel wrote that his father "was then in his forties" and that the bride was about fifteen, while the act gives thirty-six and seventeen — and it does not mention Sura Szrojd, the first wife, thirteen years earlier, at all. But the shape of the story is accurate, and one detail the memoir omits is precisely what explains why a rabbinic ruling was needed in the first place: Szmul was a widower.

According to his son's testimony, Szmul Schafir worked at roofing — straw for the poor, tiles for the houses of the nobility — and wandered from village to village around Wojsławice, as far as Chełm and Lublin; the trade and the wandering were what Mendel learned from him, from a young age.

The children, as they appear in the index

Mendel wrote that his parents had eight children — four sons and four daughters. The index of the Wojsławice registers, in which the family is recorded under the spelling SZAUFER (which is why every search under the spelling "Schafir" missed it for years), yields six of them by name9:
