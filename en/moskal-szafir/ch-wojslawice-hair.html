Moskal–Schafir · Chapter 1

Wojsławice — the town

Before the family is documented by name, it lives in a place. Wojsławice, in the Chełm district, is the stage on which everyone in this branch stood — from the community that was expelled in its entirety in 1761 to the children who were marched out of it to Sobibór in 1942.

Sources: Pinkas Hakehillot Polin · Teatr NN · Wojsławice yizkor book (1970)

A community in a private town

Wojsławice was a nobleman's town, on the trade road between Chełm and Hrubieszów. Jews are first mentioned there in 1445, and by 1717 they made up about thirty-seven per cent of the population 1. In 1531, fifteen Jewish households liable for tax were recorded; in 1564 the community numbered some one hundred and twenty-five souls; on the eve of the blood libel described below, around 1761, the community numbered about five hundred souls2.

Two names, 1736

The figures above come from tax censuses: they count Jews without calling them by name. Individual Jews of the town are indeed named far earlier — «Judka, a Jew of Wojsławice» as early as 1445, «Meir, a Jewish barber of Wojsławice» in 1588, and six names in a suit before the Crown Tribunal in Lublin in 1670. But one record from 1736, in a grod (castle-court) book of the court in Chełm, says something about them that none of the earlier ones says.

On 5 April 1736 Felicjan Zabilski, parish priest of Wojsławice, entered a manifestation against a group of men who, he claimed, had broken into the priest's house in his absence and taken movable property from it — church property among it — as well as cattle, horses and sheep. The defendants are the manager of the Wojsławice estate of Potocki, Voivode of Volhynia; the commissioner who gave the order; two members of the town council; and two Jews:

Hirszko Leybowicz and Zyskiel Doktorowicz, oppidanos Woysławicenses — townsmen of Wojsławice. …atque INFIDELES HIRSZKO LEYBOWICZ ac ZYSKIEL DOKTOROWICZ, OPPIDANOS WOYSŁAWICENSES…

⚠ And before anything else: this is a manifestation by a Catholic priest in a Christian court — an accusation, not a finding. Grod records of this kind were meant to enter one party's claim, not to decide it. Everything said below is said about what the document claims, not about what happened.

Even so, the word oppidani is worth attention: it places the two Jews as men of the town, and not as leaseholders or as Jews sheltering in the lord's court. ⚠ But it must not be over-read — the same document itself writes, thirty words later, «cum judaeis et oppidanis», «with the Jews and the townsmen», that is, it distinguishes the two categories even while it uses one word for both. And what their legal standing actually was does not follow from here: in exactly that decade Wojsławice has no independent kahal, and its Jews are apparently attached to the kahal of Chełm — and that too is an inference from silence, not a finding13.

The two councillors beside them are named Stefan and Ostap, a Polish name and a Ruthenian one — and this division echoes the town's charter, which laid down three mayors from among the Poles and one from among the Ruthenians. ◌ But two given names do not prove that the whole structure was still standing two hundred years later, and there is no document between the two.

⚠ And two further reservations. «Leybowicz» here means «son of Lejb» — a patronymic, not a surname, and the commonest in the region. One hundred and three years separate it from the marriage act of 1839 in Uchanie in which a different «Leybowicz» appears, and no connection is posited and none is implied. And no name from here has entered the tree: there is no evidence whatever linking these two men to our families14.

The town that burned

Wojsławice was a private town, and its owners are known by name. Its founding charter was granted in 1508, confirmed anew on 16 June 1598 by Mikołaj Zaklika z Czyżowa, standard-bearer of the Chełm land and owner of the town by inheritance — who explicitly confirms the founding instrument granted by «Hieronim Zaklika z Czyżowa, our grandfather» — and was entered in the grod books of Chełm only in 1753, one hundred and fifty-five years later, when a Greek Catholic priest from Krasnystaw submitted it for registration for the purposes of a church dispute15.

In that text there are no Jews at all — only two communities, Polish and Ruthenian, «Poles with Poles, Ruthenians with Ruthenians». ⚠ But absence from the document is not absence from the town: Jews are mentioned in Wojsławice as early as 1445, and are counted there in 1531 and in 1564. The privilege of 1780, granted by Humbelina Kurdwanowska née Potocka to the Jews of the town, is not their first entry but the restoration of settlement — after the expulsion of 1761.

And in between, the town burned. On 11 September 1742 the sejmik of the Chełm land resolved to exempt from tax for three years the towns that had been «ruined by fire»:

…miasta z dopuszczenia Boskiego ogniem nadruynowane… jako to: Chełm, Hrubieszow, Rejowiec, WOYSŁAWICE, UCHANIE y Krasnybrod uwalniamy…

The exact date of the fire is not known — only that it preceded September 1742. And Wojsławice and Uchanie stand there in the same line: the two towns that will recur together throughout this file, some seventy years before the family is first documented in Wojsławice, and about a hundred years before it is documented in Uchanie16.

◌ And from here a direction opens that has not yet been checked. After a fire that occurred in Lubomla in the summer of 1728, the town's two communities submitted to the grod books a list of the burned houses, house by house, by the owner's name, confirmed on oath — one hundred and three Jewish houses and twenty-two shops. Lists of this kind were submitted in order to obtain the exemption, and therefore if one was drawn up for Wojsławice too, its place is in the years that preceded 1742 — and it is in roughly 1730–1742 that it should be sought. If it exists, it will be a census of the town's Jews, house by house, from the eighteenth century. ◌ Its existence is only supposed; it is inferred from the pattern and not from a document17.

The blood libel of 1761

This affair, documented in a court record printed in that same year3, is one of the last blood-libel trials in the Kingdom of Poland. In 1760 or 1761 the body of a Christian boy of about three, Mikołaj Andrzejuk, from the nearby village of Czarnołozy, was found. The accusation of ritual murder was spread by members of the Frankist sect, who had settled in the town that year on the instruction of Jacob Frank and who sought, according to the research, to dispossess the Jews and take their place4.

The accused — the rabbis Sender Zyskielok and Herszko Józefowicz, Lejba Moszkowicz ("Sznicki") and Josa Szymulowicz — were interrogated under torture. The criminal court in Krasnystaw sentenced them to death by dismemberment while still alive; those who agreed to convert had the sentence commuted to beheading. Five of the condemned were executed in the prison square. Rabbi Józefowicz hanged himself in prison before sentence was passed; the court ruled that his body be dragged through the streets, tied to a horse's tail, and afterwards burned. All the Jews of Wojsławice were expelled from the town5.

Mendel Schafir, author of the childhood memoir that accompanies the next chapter, wrote that his father told him about the Frankist period and about noblemen of Jewish descent, and that the elders of the town in the beit midrash repeated the same things themselves6. A man born some one hundred and twenty years after the expulsion still grew up inside its memory.

The return, and the growth under Russia

In 1780 the owner of the town, Humbelina Kurdwanowska née Potocka, permitted the Jews to return and settle7. The recovery was slow: in 1778 only about thirty-eight Jews were counted. Over the course of the nineteenth century, under the Russian rule that began in 1815, the community grew steadily until it became an absolute majority in the town:

It is precisely in this window — 1826 onwards — that the town's Jewish vital registration opens, and in it are documented the five families this research follows: Moskal, Schafir, Szyfman, Kac and Glazer, all in fond 35/1787 at the State Archive in Lublin8. Two synagogues stood side by side — the old one from about 1780 and the new one, built in 1890–18949. In the town there were shtiblekh of the Trisk, Bełz and Ger Hasidim.

Between the two world wars

With the founding of independent Poland in 1918, harassment by Polish soldiers was recorded. In 1921 eight hundred and thirty-five Jews were counted, and by 1928 they made up fifty-seven per cent of the town's residents. Public life was lively: branches of Zionist movements, a Betar nest, a "Beit Yaakov" school, two libraries and four chadarim10. This is the period Mendel Schafir describes in his memoir: as a boy he heard in Chełm the first Zionist sermon to reach the region — delivered by Nachman Syrkin — and carried the news back to Wojsławice; more than thirty years later his daughter Dvora ran the Po'alei Zion evening classes in the town itself6.

The occupation and the liquidation

The German army entered the town on 11 September 1939. About one hundred and fifty Jews were sent to forced labour in the Żmudź marshes, and about eighty to the building of the Bełżec camp. The synagogue was converted into stables, and the beit midrash into a mill11. At the beginning of January 1942, fifty to sixty elderly Jews were deported by way of Włodawa to Sobibór. In October 1942 all the Jews of Wojsławice were marched out of the town and deported to Sobibór; in the same month sixty of those caught were shot in the meadows north of the market square. The community was destroyed completely12.

What this means for the family

Not one of the five men counted in the blood libel of 1761 belonged to the Moskal, Schafir, Szyfman, Kac or Glazer families — the earliest family documentation, the Bukszpan–Szyfman chain, begins only some sixty years later, in the nearby villages on the Bug (see the previous chapter). But the family that lived in the town from 1826 onwards lived inside a town of which the memory of the expulsion was still a part, and its share in the community's final fate — the deportation to Sobibór in 1942 — was no different from that of its neighbours.
