How the research was done

Method

This research does not rest on hearsay. It rests on acts — birth, marriage and death records written in Congress Poland and Austrian Galicia in the 19th century, preserved in Poland's state archives, and read one by one.

Grading certainty

Every fact on this site carries a grade, shown beside the field itself on the person's card.

Documented — an act or document read in full Probable — an index, or independent sources cross-referenced Inference — reasoning only, with no supporting document

When one document contradicts another, both versions are shown — not smoothed into one convenient story. The mark ⚠ flags an open dispute on the person's card.

The archives

The Jewish records of Congress Poland (from 1826) and of Austrian Galicia survive extensively, and most are accessible today through three sources:

  • Archiwum Państwowe w Lublinie — the registers of Wojsławice (fond 35/1787), Świerże (35/1774) and Chełm (35/1721), scanned and online.
  • JRI-Poland (legacy.jri-poland.org) — a name index with a direct link to the scan for every indexed record.
  • AGAD (the central archive in Warsaw) — the Rawa Ruska registers from Galicia.

For the Albert–Rywenbajrych family, by contrast, the Opatów registers (fond 24/44/0) are not scanned at all — there, the only route is writing to the archive directly.

The pitfalls we learned to recognise

The same traps came up again and again. They are listed here so that the next reader (or we ourselves, six months from now) will not fall into them again.

Sort orderWithin a single letter of a skorowidz (index), entries are sorted by act number — not alphabetically. You cannot skip; every line must be read.
Multiple spellingsThe same family appears under entirely different spellings — SZAUFER/Schafir, ALBERT/ALPERT/ALBIRT/ELBERT. In one Łódź act (560/1932) the groom himself signed "Albert" while the clerk wrote "Albirt" — direct proof of the phenomenon.
Misleading look-alikes"Alter" in handwriting looks almost identical to "Albert". A line read as "Chaim Albert" in an index turned out, once the act itself was read, to be "Chaim-Szlama Alter", a child from Mława — a different person entirely.
A hard documentation boundaryJewish civil registration begins in 1826. Before it, no one has a birth or marriage act — only later death acts can reveal parents from the generation before that line.
An index is not proof of absenceThe JRI index covers only some years and districts. "Not found in the index" is weak negative evidence; only reading the register itself, page by page, is strong.

The search tool

The site's search layer (Search) runs entirely locally, with no server and no external service, and includes every archival spelling alongside the Hebrew names. All the raw data is open to direct inspection as json files.
