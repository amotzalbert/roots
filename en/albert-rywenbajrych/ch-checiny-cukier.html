Albert–Rywenbajrych · Chapter 6

Chęciny and Ruda: how the Cukier family got its name

One marriage act of 1824, and the four papers filed with it, carried the Cukier line three generations further back — to Wulf and Dwojra of Chęciny — and caught in writing the moment a Jewish family in Poland exchanged a patronymic for a surname.

● Documented — all five records read from the image Source: the Strawczyn register of 1824 and its alegata, AP Kielce

Two names that would not resolve

In the Jewish register of Radoszyce, through the 1840s, two names recur in the marriage rows of six brothers and sisters: Aron Cukier, a potaźnik — potash maker — of the village of Ruda Strawczyńska, and his wife Tauba Moszkowicz. Beyond that there was nothing. No birth date, no parents, no marriage act. Who Aron's own parents were had stood open for a year1.

What closed it was not one document but five, and all of them sat in the same place: in the marriage register of the parish of Strawczyn for 1824, and in the file of supporting papers — the alegata — that went with it.

The act

On 12 January 1824, before Rev. Andrzej Groth, who served also as civil registrar of gmina Strawczyn, there married:

“…the Jew Aaron Cukroski, aged twenty-nine not yet complete, widower of Sara née Rutkowska, resident at Ruda, a potasznik, son of Mortka Cukroski, likewise a potasznik, formerly resident at Ruda, and of Esterka née Chojna; and there appeared also the Jewish maiden Taube Potasznicka, aged twenty-two, in service at Ruda, daughter of Mośko Potasznicki and Esterka née Lewek, with her mother at her side…” …Starozakonny Aaron Cukroski […] Wdowiec po Sarze z Rutkowskich, w Rudzie zamieszkały Potasznik, Syn Mortki Cukroskiego Potasznika niegdy w Rudzie zamieszkałego i Esterki z Choynow…

At the foot of the page, below the priest's signature, stand two lines in Hebrew letters. The first reads “Aharon Cukrowski” — the groom signed for himself. The second reads “Shlomo Rutkovski”, one of the witnesses, a potash maker at Ruda aged forty-five, who bears the surname of Aron's dead first wife. The act accounts for exactly those two signatures: it was “signed by us, by the newly-wed and by one of the witnesses”, everyone else present being unable to write2.

What was in the file

The alegata of 1824 survives entire — thirty-seven pages — and the papers of act no. 1 sit at the front of it. Four of them:

The Chęciny kahal certificate for Aron, 29 December 1823, signed for the Synagogue Board by Mendel Königsztein and carrying a round seal with a crown, the Tablets of the Law and Hebrew lettering. It gives his birth to the day: “born here in the town of Chęciny… on the 21st day of February in the year one thousand seven hundred and ninety-five”. And it adds one clause that explains everything that happened that year: his parents, “who died only this year”.

The death act of Sara, his first wife — 20 September 1823, at Ruda, in house number sixty, aged twenty-eight.

The death act of Mortka himself, from Chęciny — and this is the document that carried the line two generations up. He died in house number forty-four, aged seventy-one, and the act names his own parents: “son of Wulf and Dwojra Cukrowscy… of the town of Chęciny, both already dead”. The first declarant was his son Joachym Cukrowski, a trader in Chęciny — a brother of Aron whose existence was unknown until this document was read3.

The Chęciny kahal certificate for Toba, 4 January 1824: born 8 December 1801 in Chęciny, daughter of Moszek Szmulowicz and Ester Lewkówna.

The moment surnames were born

Both Chęciny certificates use the same formula, and both say the same thing: “na teraz … zwanego” — now called. Not “whose name is”, not “of the family”, but now called. This is the surname law of the Kingdom of Poland of 1821, caught in writing two or three years after it took effect, on one family:

The old patronymicThe new surname
Aron Mordkowicz — son of Mordka→ Cukrowski
the Wulfowicze — the house of Mortka son of Wulf→ Cukrowscy
Toba Moszkówna — daughter of Moszek→ Potasznicka
Moszek Szmulowicz · Ester Lewkówna→ Potaszniccy

And even then the name had not settled. Within three months of 1823 Mortka is written Cukrowicz in September at Strawczyn, Cukrowski in October at Chęciny, and Cukroski — another spelling again — in January 1824. By 1844, in the Radoszyce register, the family is Cukier. One family, four spellings, twenty years.

Neither new surname is arbitrary. Cukier is sugar, and Potasznicki is the potash maker. Moszek, Tauba's father, was himself a potasznik, and so were Aron and Mortka. The new name is, quite literally, the trade.

What is still open

In Sara's death act the first declarant is called “Mortka Cukrowicz, seventy, father of the deceased”. But that same Mortka is Aron's father — the Chęciny certificate and the marriage act both say so. Were he also Sara's father, Aron would have married his sister. That is impossible. The reading that survives is that the clerk wrote “father” where he meant father-in-law; settling it requires the original act, not the certified copy in the file4.

Beyond that, a new front has opened. Both age certificates were issued by the kahal of Chęciny, and both spouses were born there. Aron's birth in 1795 and Toba's in 1801 predate civil registration in the Kingdom, so they stand in no civil register; but the Chęciny books are where the marriage of Mortka and Ester, the death of Ester, and Joachym's family now stand waiting.

Postscript, 29 August: the original act at Chęciny

What was read first was the certified extract filed for the wedding. A day later the Chęciny books themselves opened, and in them stands the original — act 220 of 1823, karta 318, found through the volume's own alphabetical death index: “30 · 220 · Cukrowski Mortka · lat 71 · karta 318”. It confirms the extract word for word, and adds two things.

The date is settled. In the extract the month was not fully legible; in the original it is plain — “Dnia trzydziestego Października”. The act is of 30 October 1823, and the death of the day before, 29 October, at seven in the evening.

And they signed in Hebrew. The act closes “Po czem Oświadczaiący PODPISALI PO HEBRAYSZKU” — and two Hebrew signatures stand beneath it. The first reads “Chaim Cukrowski”. The Hebrew name of the man the Polish clerk calls Joachym was Chaim — and this is the handwriting of Aron's brother.

⚠ And a correction. From the certificate's clause “who died only this year” I concluded that Estera died in 1823. That was an over-read. The Chęciny death index for 1823 has now been read in full — 288 deaths — and Estera Cukrowska is not in it. All that is certain is that she was already dead on 29 December 1823, the day the certificate was written. Where and when she died is open.
