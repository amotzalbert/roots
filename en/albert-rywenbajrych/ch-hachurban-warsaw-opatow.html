Albert–Rywenbajrych · Chapter 5

The Destruction in Warsaw and Opatów

The two communities this branch came from — Warsaw and Opatów — were both destroyed. Chaim and Chana survived; Chana's parents, Tuvia and Bajla-Szajndla, did not.

● documented — public sources on the fate of the two communities

Warsaw: Leszno inside the ghetto

Warsaw surrendered to the German army on 28 September 1939, after bombardments that badly hit the northern Jewish quarter; anyone living at Leszno 36 in those weeks — as Chaim Albert did — experienced the siege at the centre of the bombing1. In November 1940 the street, as far as Żelazna Street, was enclosed within the Warsaw ghetto established around it; more than 400,000 people were packed into the ghetto on less than two and a half percent of the city's area — hunger, typhus and overcrowding killed tens of thousands even before the deportations1. Between 22 July and 21 September 1942 about a quarter of a million of the ghetto's Jews were deported to the Treblinka extermination camp. On 19 April 1943 the Warsaw ghetto uprising broke out; on 16 May Jürgen Stroop blew up the Great Synagogue as a sign of "victory", and the quarter — Muranów within it, Leszno on its edge — was razed to the ground1. After the war a new district was built on the ruins along a completely different street layout; most of the historic addresses, Leszno 36 among them, no longer exist.

Opatów: the ghetto and its liquidation

The Germans took Opatów in September 1939, set fire to the market area, and locked between one thousand and one thousand five hundred inhabitants — Poles and Jews — in the cinema building for two days without food; about two hundred young Jewish men were seized and did not return2. On 6 April 1941 an open ghetto was established, its population reaching some ten thousand at its peak, including refugees from Warsaw, Łódź and Vienna. The Judenrat was headed by "M. Wajsblum" — there is no documentation explicitly linking him to the Wajsblum family of this branch, but the name and the place coincide, and this is a point that deserves further inquiry, not a conclusion2.

On 20–22 October 1942, as part of "Operation Reinhard", German gendarmerie, SS men and Ukrainian auxiliaries surrounded the ghetto. Some six thousand five hundred men, women and children were assembled in Targowica Square and marched about eighteen kilometres to the railway station at Jasice; those who weakened on the way were beaten and shot. About one hundred and twenty people were packed into each freight car; the journey to Treblinka took about three days without food or water, and most of the deportees were murdered in the gas chambers on arrival. The total deported to Treblinka is estimated at seven thousand to eight thousand; about two thousand were sent to labour camps — Sandomierz, Starachowice, Radom and Skarżysko-Kamienna2. The number of survivors from the community as a whole — only about three hundred, out of a community of some eight thousand2.

Tuvia and Bajla-Szajndla — Treblinka

Tuvia Rywenbajrych and his wife Bajla-Szajndla née Wajsblum, Chana's parents, were murdered in the Holocaust. The list of the murdered in The Jews of Opatow (Tel Aviv, 1988), the town's memorial book compiled by its survivors, records both of them by name: "Tomasz Rywenbajrych, born 1888, permanent resident of Opatów, died Treblinka 23 October 1942", and beside him "Bajla-Szajndla Rywenbajrych, born 1891"3 — one day after the end of the ghetto's liquidation. However, the 1988 list attaches that same date and place to almost everyone it names, and in one documented case it has been refuted — so the precise date is a probable reconstruction, not documentation. The list gives Bajla-Szajndla under her married surname — independent confirmation of the marriage. For both of them Chana, their daughter, submitted Pages of Testimony at Yad Vashem4.

Fajga-Rachel — Skarżysko-Kamienna

Fajga-Rachel, Chana's younger sister, born 1920/21, was not murdered with her parents. The Page of Testimony that Chana submitted for her states: place of death — Skarżysko-Kamienna, cause of death — shooting4. The 1988 list places her, together with her parents, at Treblinka on 23 October — but a contemporary German document, the lists of Skarżysko-Kamienna prisoners marked "sick" between November 1942 and February 1943, carries her name: that is, she was alive in the camp after the date given in the list5. A document written at the time takes precedence over a list compiled by survivors in 1988, whose authors apparently assumed that the whole household perished in the October deportation. In the camp, the marking "sick" served in practice as a selection list.

Chana herself survived, and is documented alive in the same camp in those same months.
