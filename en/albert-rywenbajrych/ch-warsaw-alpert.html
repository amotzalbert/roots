Albert–Rywenbajrych · Chapter 1

Jewish Warsaw: the Alpert family

The anchor document for the whole branch: a single marriage act from 1894, read in full from the original scan — not from an index — which turned out to hold the moment when the surname "Alpert" became, in the same document and on the same day, also "Albert".

● documented — the act was read in full Source: marriage act 75/1894, Warsaw, Cyrkuł V

The act

On 6/18 February 1894 (the double date — Julian/Gregorian — was the Russian registration convention of the period) Dawid Alpert and Rudla Butkowska married in Warsaw. The act itself, read at full resolution from a scan of the original register, states the following explicitly1:

“Dawid Alpert, son of Abram and Estera-Gitla née Cuker, some twenty-five years of age, resident of the city of Warsaw…” Давидъ Альпертъ, сынъ Абрама и Эстеры-Гитли урожденной Цукеръ, двадцати [пяти] лѣтъ, житель города Варшавы… Warsaw Archive, Cyrkuł V, marriage act 75/1894, fond 72/200/0.

Two details come from this line alone: Abram and Estera-Gitla née Cuker, Dawid's parents, are a newly opened generation; and Dawid's age — about twenty-five — places his birth around 1869. And one small, legible sign in the Russian wording proves more than it appears to: the act does not carry the word "the late" (покойнаго) before the parents' names, whereas the standard Russian formula requires it explicitly when a parent is no longer living. Abram and Estera-Gitla Alpert were alive in 1894.

“The bride, the maiden Rudla Butkowska, daughter of Abram and Ruchla — twenty-one years of age… of the gmina of Piątek in the Kalisz gubernia, residing temporarily in Warsaw” Невѣста дѣвица Рудля Бутковская дочь Абрама и Рухли — двадцати одного года, …Гмины Пiонтки Калишской губернiи, временно въ Варшавѣ проживающая. Ibid., act 75/1894.

Rudla, his bride, therefore came not from Warsaw itself but from the gmina of Piątek — a small town in the Kalisz gubernia — and was only "temporarily" in Warsaw: a familiar marriage pattern, a city man marrying a small-town woman who had come to work or to go into service in the capital. Her father, Abram, and her mother, Ruchla — but Ruchla's own maiden name was not recorded in the act. The clerk, when he knew a maiden name, wrote it out explicitly — as he had done a line earlier for Estera-Gitla. In Ruchla's case, after "daughter of Abram and Ruchla", there is only a dash. This is not an omission by the index — the information simply was not recorded in the original act1. It was eventually found somewhere else entirely: in the Piątek registers themselves, where Ruchla is recorded under her maiden name Rochberger2.

⚠ And a further, larger correction: for months the bride's surname was read here as "Buzowska". A re-reading of the same scan, at eight times magnification, showed that the letter is т and not ж — Butkowska. The correction opened up three generations in Piątek, and is described in the next chapter2.

The witnesses, and the neighbourhood

The two marriage witnesses — Szmaj Wajnberg, about fifty-seven, of Ostrowska Street, and Abram Rankel (the reading of the name is only probable), aged twenty-nine, of Smocza Street — lived on two streets in Muranów, the Jewish quarter of Warsaw. That anchors the couple not only to the city but to the specific neighbourhood. At the foot of the page Dawid Alpert signed in his own hand, "Давидъ Альпертъ", and beside it a signature in Hebrew.

Alpert and Albert, in the same document

At the head of the act the clerk wrote "Альпертъ" — with a p. In the closing paragraph, summing up the ceremony, the same clerk, on the same page, wrote:

“…between the aforementioned groom, Dawid Albert, and the maiden Rudla Butkowska, and that this marriage was performed…” …между вышеупомянутыми Женихомъ Давида Альберта съ дѣвицей Рудлей Бутковской, и что бракъ сей совершился… Ibid., act 75/1894, closing paragraph.

The same act, the same clerk, the same day — and yet two spellings: Alpert at the opening, Albert at the close. Until this discovery it was assumed that "Albert" was a form created in Israel after the family's immigration. The name was fluid long before that: already in 1894, in Warsaw, a Russian clerk alternated between the forms in the middle of one official document. Chaim Albert, registered under that form in Israel, was continuing a form that already existed in the family — he did not invent it.
