Albert–Rywenbajrych · Chapter 4

Chaim and Chana

The two branches — the Warsaw one and the Opatów one — meet here. Wartime records place Chaim and Chana in the same camp, Skarżysko-Kamienna, at approximately the same period — both of them in documentation written at the time.

● documented — CKZP registration slips, Polish citizenship confirmation 2015, the Arolsen tracing-file index of 1947

Chaim at Leszno 36

Chaim Albert, the son of Dawid Alpert and Rudla, was a "czapnik" — a capmaker. According to the registration cards of the Central Committee of Polish Jews (CKZP) kept by the family, in 1939 he was living at 36 Leszno Street in Warsaw1 — a street about eighty percent of whose residents were Jewish in 1938, in the heart of the north-western Jewish quarter2. The street was badly hit in the bombardments of September 1939; from November 1940 it was enclosed — as far as Żelazna Street — within the Warsaw ghetto established around it2. The building he lived in, Leszno 36, no longer exists: the whole quarter was razed to the ground after the ghetto uprising of 1943, and after the war the street layout was changed completely2.

Two documents, two years

Chaim Albert's year of birth is not certain. Two CKZP cards give two different years — 1907 and 1913 — and both are self-declarations made after the war, not independent birth documents. The Polish citizenship application the family submitted in 2015 repeats 1907, but that does not decide between the pieces of evidence — it only establishes which of the two declarations the family in fact committed to3. An index card of the Arolsen Archives from 1947 gives the date 7 November 19077. A search of the Warsaw birth records for both dates produced a double negative: the Jewish birth register of Warsaw for 1907 did not survive in the archive, and the index of acts for 1913 contains no Alpert, Albert or Elbert by the name of Chaim3.

The war: Częstochowa, Skarżysko-Kamienna

The CKZP cards document his route: from Leszno 36 in Warsaw, by way of Częstochowa, to the Skarżysko-Kamienna camp in 1942 — and finally to 7 Targowa Street1. Chana Rywenbajrych, the daughter of Tuvia and Bajla-Szajndla, appears in lists of Jewish prisoners at Skarżysko-Kamienna, marked "sick", between November 1942 and February 19434 — the first time she is documented in a record written at the time, and not only in testimony given years afterwards.

The two separate sources — Chaim's CKZP card and the list of sick prisoners carrying Chana's name — place both of them in the same camp, Skarżysko-Kamienna, at approximately the same period4.

After the war: Łódź, then Israel

Chaim registered in Warsaw on 14 August 1945, at 7 Targowa Street, having already received an identity document from the city committee on 12 March of that year; Chana registered in Łódź in January 19461. His name also appears in the Arolsen list “List of Surviving Jews, Registered in Lodz” — “Chaim Albert, born 1913, Warsaw”7. In 1947 the Arolsen Archives record a joint tracing request by the couple: two index cards with consecutive file numbers — 705603 in the name of Chaim Albert, and 705604 in the name of “Chana [Sara] Albert née Rywenbajrych”, born 18 June 1917 — the date that appears also in the 2015 citizenship application, and the name “Sara” that appears also on her gravestone7. This is the first documentation of the couple together in a record outside the family's own papers. They married after the war and at first remained in Poland: Bella was born in Łódź on 15 July 1946, and David, Amotz's father, on 20 June 1951 — both in Łódź13. Their address in Łódź was 44/51 Kilińskiego Street. All four emigrated to Israel around 1956, when David was about five — in the large wave of emigration of Polish Jews of 1956–1957. Chaim continued to work in cap manufacture.

Sharing the apartment: the Kiestenbaum family

At exactly the same address — Łódź, Kilińskiego 44/51 — the Kiestenbaum family was also registered in July 1946: Sara Kiestenbaum née Szejnauk, born in Tarnobrzeg in 1901, her husband Pejsach, and their twins Hersz and Izrael, born 1939. They survived the war in Krasnoyarsk in Siberia1. Sara's mother was named Blima Wajsblum — Chana's mother's family name. The Kiestenbaum family is documented in Opatów throughout the nineteenth century, and the Opatów index has a Bluma-Necha Wajsblum born in 1873, an age that fits Sara's mother exactly6. The two families who shared an apartment in Łódź in 1946 were, apparently, relatives on Chana's side — but the exact connection has not been proved.

His death

Chaim Albert died on 12 September 1976 — 17 Elul 5736 — in Holon5. During the research a contradiction stood for a time against a reading of the gravestone that gave 17 Adar I 5737 (7 March 1977); it was checked and resolved in favour of the date in the register. His grave in Holon is known and documented in a photograph.
